\section{Introduction}
Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation was proposed to reach deep brain targets while
reducing stimulation of surrounding tissue \cite{grossman2017}. It applies two sinusoids at
$f_1$ and $f_2=f_1+\Delta f$ through separate electrode pairs, typically with
$f_1\sim1$--$2$~kHz and $\Delta f\sim1$--$100$~Hz. Where the fields overlap, their
superposition is an amplitude-modulated (AM) signal with an envelope at $\Delta f$.
Electrode currents can steer the region of largest envelope modulation. Whether this
also localizes the neural response depends on how neurons transduce the waveform.

The difference frequency is absent from the input itself:
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\varepsilon_1\cos(\omega_1 t)+\varepsilon_2\cos(\omega_2 t),\qquad
\omega_{1,2}=2\pi f_{1,2},\quad \Omega=\omega_2-\omega_1=2\pi\,\Delta f .
\label{eq:am}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_{1,2}$ are the effective input amplitudes (related to the applied field
in \S\ref{sec:coupling}), $\Omega$ is the beat angular frequency, and
$f_c=(f_1+f_2)/2$ is the carrier frequency. The spectrum contains only the two applied
lines (Fig.~\ref{fig:demod}). A linear, time-invariant system can attenuate or phase-shift
them but cannot create a new one. Passive membrane filtering therefore cannot
demodulate TI \cite{mirzakhalili2020}; a nonlinear element must mix the carriers into
the low-frequency band, as in AM reception \cite{nahin2001}.

The mechanism therefore has distinct physical requirements (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}).
\emph{Access} is the conversion of the extracellular field into local membrane
polarization. \emph{Detection}, or demodulation, is the nonlinear generation of the
difference-frequency component from those polarizations. \emph{Amplification} is
the response of the neuron and its recurrent circuit to the generated component.
The same cellular structures can contribute to more than one stage, but strong
coupling, effective mixing, and large resonant gain are different properties.

Access depends on the compartment that receives the field. A somatic charging time
of $10$--$30$~ms strongly attenuates kHz polarization \cite{deitcher2017,eyal2016},
whereas axonal geometry and myelination support different voltage responses.
Cohen et al.\ \cite{cohen2020periaxonal} established a conducting periaxonal space
beneath myelin that separates nodal and internodal voltage dynamics and supports
saltatory conduction. This provides an anatomical basis for considering access
through fast axonal compartments rather than assigning every compartment the
somatic filter. Nodes and the axon initial segment (AIS) are candidate sites; the
AIS also has a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels \cite{kole2008ais}.
We represent this candidate route by a shorter effective membrane time. Its
quantitative transfer from an applied TI field to local polarization remains to
be calibrated; the measured and assumed time scales are distinguished in
Table~\ref{tab:doors}.

Detection requires that this polarization encounter a nonlinearity. Mirzakhalili
et al.\ \cite{mirzakhalili2020} demonstrated subthreshold nodal mixing in a modified
McIntyre--\allowbreak Richardson--\allowbreak Grill (MRG) myelinated-axon model
with Hodgkin--Huxley ion-channel dynamics. Cortical-neuron models also
show axonal excitation and conduction block under stronger fields \cite{wang2023}.
Luff et al.\ \cite{luff2024mixer} directly observed subthreshold difference-frequency
oscillations in individual neurons, including carriers near $5$~kHz. Mixing persisted
during synaptic blockade but was suppressed by blocking tetrodotoxin-sensitive sodium
channels. These measurements establish a cellular detector without locating it
within a specific compartment. In a population description, smooth cellular
contributions can be combined into an effective second-order response; a static
rate curve realizes that response through its curvature.

Amplification determines whether the generated beat appreciably changes activity.
Intrinsic conductances can tune a single neuron \cite{hutcheonyarom2000}, as in the
cell-type- and state-dependent TI responses reported by de Palma Aristides et al.\
\cite{depalma2026ti}. Recurrent excitatory--inhibitory circuits provide a separate
source of resonance \cite{brunelhakim1999,brunelwang2003,ledoux2011}.
In slices, pyramidal and parvalbumin (PV) neurons respond differently,
and most pyramidal TI responses disappear under synaptic blockade \cite{caldas2024}.
Computational studies also find network-mediated responses
\cite{karimi2024,soroushi2025,wangw2026}. Primate recordings at human-comparable
fields report changes in spike timing without a corresponding mean-rate change,
with weaker effects than conventional low-frequency stimulation \cite{vieira2024}.
Cellular mixing and circuit amplification can therefore contribute to the same
response.

We focus on weak perturbations of ongoing neural activity. Neurons may already be
spiking; weakness refers to the stimulation-induced change around that operating
state. A smooth response with a nonzero second-order term gives the leading beat,
and linear amplification further requires stability under the rectified mean shift.
Other preparations show carrier-driven excitation or block rather than envelope
extraction \cite{opancar2025,peripheral2023,semenov2025}. Direct carrier-evoked
spiking and conduction block lie outside the weak-input calculation;
finite-amplitude network examples are identified separately.

The first calculation uses a Jansen--Rit firing-rate sigmoid:
its curvature mixes the carriers, and a network with second-order synapses amplifies
the beat near a Hopf bifurcation. An exact quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) mean
field connects this static description to cellular dynamics. When membrane relaxation
is fast relative to the input, a static population transfer curve emerges while the
synapses remain dynamic; otherwise the mixing coefficient is frequency dependent.
Simulations in the corresponding spiking network test the population description,
and a laminar model extends the calculation to two resonant bands at low carriers.

Related neural-mass accounts use the same nonlinear mixing for endogenous
cross-frequency coupling and nested amplitude modulation
\cite{ruffini2025cfc,ruffini2025ham}. Here the input is external, making the
field-to-cell coupling an additional requirement.

\section{Theory}

\subsection{Population transfer and the slow-synapse limit}\label{sec:nmm}
A neural mass model describes a population through averaged variables
\cite{ruffini2025rosetta,jansen1995}. In the Jansen--Rit (JR) family, a linear synaptic
filter converts presynaptic firing rate $r(t)$ into postsynaptic potential $x(t)$:
\[
\ddot x+2a\dot x+a^2x=Aa\,r(t),
\]
with inverse synaptic time $a$ and gain parameter $A$; inhibitory synapses use
parameters $b$ and $B$. Summed postsynaptic potentials give the mean input $v$, which
a static transfer curve converts to firing rate \cite{wilsoncowan1972},
\begin{equation}
\Sig(v)=\frac{2e_0}{1+e^{\,\rho(v_0-v)}} .
\label{eq:sigmoid}
\end{equation}
Here $2e_0$ is the maximum rate, $v_0$ the inflection voltage, and $\rho$ the inverse
voltage scale. Nonzero curvature makes this curve a mixer, while its slope sets
the local gain of the recurrent circuit.

For a synapse from population $Y$ to $X$, the resulting NMM1 equations are
\cite{ruffini2025rosetta}
\begin{equation}
\hat L_{X\leftarrow Y}\big[u_{X\leftarrow Y}\big]=C_{X\leftarrow Y}\,r_Y,\qquad
v_X=\Lambda[\hat E]+\!\!\sum_{Y:\,C_{X\leftarrow Y}\neq0}\!\!u_{X\leftarrow Y},\qquad
r_X=\Sig(v_X).
\label{eq:nmm1}
\end{equation}
The operator $\hat L$ is the inverse synaptic filter, $u_{X\leftarrow Y}$ its
postsynaptic potential, $C_{X\leftarrow Y}$ the connectivity, and $\Lambda[\hat E]$
the direct polarization caused by the applied field. This field contribution enters
alongside the synaptic potentials, without first passing through a synapse.

The corresponding population nonlinearity can be derived for all-to-all coupled
QIF neurons with Lorentzian-distributed excitabilities. Their exact
Montbri\'o--Paz\'o--Roxin (MPR) mean field evolves the firing rate $r$ and mean voltage
$v$ \cite{montbrio2015}; adding dynamic synapses gives NMM2
\cite{ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}. In membrane-time units,
\begin{equation}
\dot r=\frac{\Delta}{\pi}+2rv,\qquad
\dot v=v^2+\bar\eta-\pi^2r^2+I(t),
\label{eq:mpr}
\end{equation}
where $\bar\eta$ is the mean excitability, $\Delta>0$ its half-width, and $I(t)$ the
normalized input, including recurrent drive when present. Rate and voltage are
expressed in the corresponding rescaled units. For a constant total drive
$Q=\bar\eta+I$, setting both derivatives to zero gives $v=-\Delta/(2\pi r)$ and
\begin{equation}
r=F(Q)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{Q+\sqrt{Q^2+\Delta^2}}{2}} .
\label{eq:Fmpr}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $F$ averages the single-neuron steady rate $f(q)=\sqrt q/\pi$ for
$q>0$, and $f(q)=0$ otherwise, over the excitability distribution. Here $\eta$ is
the neuron's excitability and $q=\eta+I$ its constant total drive. Threshold
recruitment and the concave rate increase of active cells both enter its curvature. This nonlinear curve exists
without recurrent coupling. It has an inflection at
$Q=\Delta/\sqrt3$ and an unbounded concave rise, unlike the saturating JR sigmoid.

If the population relaxes to this equilibrium faster than synaptic inputs change,
NMM2 reduces to a static rate map with dynamic synapses
\cite{ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}. Restoring the membrane time $\tau_m$ and physical
firing rate, a second-order synapse with time constant $\tau_s$ obeys
\begin{equation}
r(t)\simeq \frac{F(Q(t))}{\tau_m},\qquad
\tau_s^2\ddot s_{\rm syn}+2\tau_s\dot s_{\rm syn}+s_{\rm syn}=r(t),
\label{eq:slow_synapse}
\end{equation}
where $s_{\rm syn}$ is the filtered rate entering the recurrent drive in $Q$, with the original
coupling normalization retained. This is the slow-synapse limit: shortening the
effective membrane time makes the transfer appear static while the network retains
its synaptic dynamics. The relevant separation is between the population relaxation
time $\tau_{\rm rel}$ and $\tau_s$. Applying the same static map directly to a
carrier of angular frequency $\omega_c$ requires the stronger condition
$\omega_c\tau_{\rm rel}\ll1$. A population can therefore be quasistatic for its
synaptic inputs yet dynamic for a kHz carrier.

The transfer curve summarizes cellular nonlinearities within this regime. Its
curvature combines their leading contributions to mixing; its slope converts a
slow input into a rate change and enters recurrent gain. QIF supplies a derived
realization through its spike-generating quadratic and excitability distribution.
The generic weak-input mixing law does not require identifying each channel's
contribution, although the coefficient and its frequency dependence are
model-specific. Outside the static limit these contributions belong to a dynamic
input--output functional. Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} calculates this response for
QIF and recovers Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fmpr} in the adiabatic limit.

Cellular and circuit resonance are also distinct. Conductances such as $I_h$ and
$I_M$ can tune single neurons \cite{hutcheonyarom2000}; the companion manuscript of
de Palma Aristides et al.\ \cite{depalma2026ti} reports cell-type- and state-dependent
TI frequency selectivity in hippocampal neurons, sensitive to these conductances.
Recurrent excitatory--inhibitory circuits can generate resonance without intrinsic
cellular resonance \cite{brunelhakim1999,brunelwang2003}. In the fast-membrane limit,
any retained resonance belongs to the remaining synaptic circuit; modes requiring
finite membrane dynamics need not persist.

The JR column couples pyramidal, excitatory-interneuron, and inhibitory-interneuron
populations, with field input to the pyramidal sigmoid and local field potential
(LFP) proxy $v=y_1-y_2$ (App.~\ref{app:models}). The laminar extension (LaNMM)
\cite{ruffini2025cfc,sanchezTodo2023} adds coupled alpha and gamma circuits: deep
pyramidal $P_1$, spiny-stellate (SS), and somatostatin (SST) populations, and a
superficial pyramidal $P_2$/PV circuit. These models retain dynamic synapses and
static rate curves; NMM2 and its spiking realization retain membrane dynamics as well.

\subsection{From applied field to a squared-envelope beat component}\label{sec:coupling}
We use the quasistatic electromagnetic approximation for the applied field $E(t)$
(V/m), neglecting propagation and induction. This does not make membrane charging
instantaneous. Let $s(t)$ be the polarization after the cellular access response;
the static population model reads
\begin{equation}
r(t)=\Sig\!\big(v(t)+s(t)\big),\qquad
E(t)=E_1\cos\omega_1 t+E_2\cos\omega_2 t .
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
The field acts directly on soma, dendrites, AIS, nodes, and terminals,
each with its own coupling and frequency response. In a reduced description, their
contributions to the effective input are represented by parallel entry points,
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\sum_c \boldsymbol\lambda^c_f\!\cdot\!\mathbf E(t),\qquad
\boldsymbol\lambda^c_f=\boldsymbol\lambda^c_0\,\lvert H_c(\omega_c)\rvert,
\label{eq:doors}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\lambda^c_0$ is the static polarization vector of compartment $c$ (a length),
$H_c$ its access filter, and $\omega_c=2\pi f_c$
\cite{ruffini2025rosetta,galangadea2023}. Equation~\eqref{eq:doors} uses amplitude
gains and assumes aligned compartment phases; a general sum requires the complex
transfers, including their relative phases. The passive
compartment equation $C\dot{\mathbf V}+A\mathbf V=\mathbf I^0-\mathbf pE$
\cite{ruffini2021nmm2} gives
$\delta\mathbf V(\omega)=-(A+i\omega C)^{-1}\mathbf pE(\omega)$, with capacitance
matrix $C$, conductance matrix $A$, endogenous current $\mathbf I^0$, and field-to-current
coupling $\mathbf p$. The static gain and its frequency dependence belong to this
one response. A model driven by post-coupling polarization and a model driven by
current through its own membrane equation must therefore count that filtering once.

For a dominant entry point, $\varepsilon_j=\lambda_f(\omega_j)E_j$ denotes the
polarization reaching the nonlinearity. The direct-coupling calculation takes
$\lambda_f\approx\lambda_0$ to isolate mixing and recurrent amplification;
\S\ref{sec:khz} restores frequency-dependent access. Fast axonal access motivates
this limit; compartment anatomy enters the reduced model through
the coupling and response time, which must be calibrated for the preparation.

Expanding $\Sig$ about an operating point $v^*$ (a Volterra/Taylor expansion, as
in \cite{ruffini2025ham}) gives
$\Sig(v^*+s)\approx\Sig(v^*)+\Sig'(v^*)\,s+\half\Sig''(v^*)\,s^2+\dots$
The truncation requires $|s|\ll1/\rho\approx1.8$~mV and a nonzero second derivative
for quadratic mixing to be leading. Larger excursions require the full transfer curve.
The linear term preserves the two input frequencies. The quadratic cross term
generates their difference through the identity
$2\cos\omega_1t\cos\omega_2t=\cos\Omega t+\cos(\omega_1+\omega_2)t$.
Averaging over the carrier (the fast
$\cos^2\omega_jt$ terms average to $\half$, the sum-frequency term to zero),
\begin{equation}
\big\langle\Sig(v^*+s)\big\rangle_c
=\Sig(v^*)+\tfrac14\Sig''(v^*)\,(\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2)
+\tfrac12\Sig''(v^*)\,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos\Omega t+O(\varepsilon^4),
\label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
so a line appears at exactly the envelope frequency $\Omega$, with amplitude
\begin{equation}
A_\Omega\simeq\half\,\Sig''(v^*)\,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 ,
\label{eq:demod}
\end{equation}
where $\simeq$ means to leading order in the polarizations, here and in what follows. The
same expansion leaves a constant (DC) term, $\tfrac14\Sig''(\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2)$,
which any read-out of the mean rate must include.

For a dynamic cellular response with synaptic input held fixed, define the
effective second-order coefficient $\chi^{(2)}$ by the complex beat amplitude
$\widehat r_\Omega$,
\[
\widehat r_\Omega\simeq\chi^{(2)}(\omega_2,-\omega_1)\,
\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_1,\qquad
r_\Omega(t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left[\widehat r_\Omega e^{i\Omega t}\right].
\]
Here $r_\Omega(t)$ is the generated rate component before recurrent feedback.
With this cosine-amplitude convention, the memoryless limit is
$\chi^{(2)}=\Sig''(v^*)/2$. Multiple cellular processes can contribute to the same
coefficient. Small amplitude justifies the second-order expansion; it does not
remove memory, so the coefficient may depend on both frequencies and the
operating state. Symmetry or cancellation can make it vanish. QIF and the sigmoid
realize this shared quadratic structure with different coefficients.

What the quadratic term recovers is not the envelope but its square. Writing $A(t)$ for
the instantaneous envelope of the two-tone field,
$A^2(t)=\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2+2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos\Omega t$, the
low-pass part of the squared signal is exactly
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LPF}\big[s^2(t)\big]=\half A^2(t)
=\frac{\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2}{2}+\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos\Omega t .
\label{eq:sqenv}
\end{equation}
This is square-law detection in the strict sense: the population transduces $A^2$, not
$A$. The distinction matters because $A^2$ is exactly sinusoidal at $\Omega$
whereas $A$ is not---at a matched target ($\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2=\varepsilon$),
$A(t)=2\varepsilon|\cos(\Omega t/2)|$, a rectified cosine.

\emph{The amplitude-modulated surrogate.} The dense perturbative sweeps below use the
three-line waveform
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\varepsilon\,[\,1+m\cos\Omega t\,]\cos\omega_c t,\qquad \omega_c=2\pi f_c ,
\label{eq:amsur}
\end{equation}
with modulation depth $m$, because it parameterizes a sweep by one amplitude. It is not
the physical signal: it carries a line at $\omega_c$ that the two-tone sum lacks. The two
agree at $\Omega$ to leading order (the surrogate gives
$A_\Omega=\half\Sig''\varepsilon^2m$) and are \emph{not} equivalent beyond it. For matched genuine tones $\mathrm{LPF}[s^2]=\varepsilon^2(1+\cos\Omega t)$,
whereas for the $m=1$ AM surrogate
$\mathrm{LPF}[s^2]=\varepsilon^2(\tfrac34+\cos\Omega t+\tfrac14\cos2\Omega t)$: the same
$\Omega$ amplitude, but a different DC term and a spurious second harmonic at $25\%$ of
the fundamental. Since that second harmonic is the observable that
distinguishes transduction classes (\S\ref{sec:transduction}), every claim that turns on
the waveform is run with genuine two tones: the demodulation demonstration
(Fig.~\ref{fig:demod}), the curvature and square-law controls (Fig.~\ref{fig:opp}), the
laminar maps (Figs.~\ref{fig:lanmm_p2},~\ref{fig:lanmm_p1}) and the kHz demonstration.
The AM surrogate is retained for the dense perturbative sweeps in $(\Omega,p)$ and
$(\Omega,C)$, which read only the leading $\Omega$ component, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:opp}d verifies their open-loop agreement at one operating point.
It does not establish quantitative equivalence of the recurrent sweeps near the Hopf,
where the different DC terms can shift the operating state.

In the language of \cite{ruffini2025ham}, the quadratic cross-term $s_1 s_2$ between a
slow and a fast input (the second-order Volterra kernel) implements amplitude
modulation/demodulation and the associated intermodulation lines.
Demodulation is thus itself an intermodulation product: the line at $\Omega$ is the
\emph{difference frequency} of the two applied tones ($\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$),
generated by the same quadratic term that, acting again on an intrinsic
rhythm $\omega_0$, produces the $\omega_0\pm\Omega$ sidebands seen at the spiking level
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}b).
Equation~\eqref{eq:demod} carries three predictions, all verified below: the
recovered amplitude is quadratic in the drive ($A_\Omega\propto\varepsilon^2$,
square-law detection); it follows the sigmoid curvature
($A_\Omega\propto\Sig''(v^*)$, hence zero at the inflection $v^*=v_0$); and, \emph{at
fixed post-coupling polarization} $\varepsilon$, it is independent of the carrier
$\omega_c$. This last prediction needs care: as a function of the \emph{applied} field the
response inherits the coupling $\varepsilon=\lambda_f E_0$, so the field required for a
given effect rises with $f_c$ (\S\ref{sec:khz})---consistent with the experimental
consensus. Carrier independence is thus a statement about the recovered beat component at fixed
polarization, not about the applied field.

\subsection{The exact mean field connects static and dynamic mixing}\label{sec:dynamic}
The adiabatic condition in \S\ref{sec:nmm} determines whether the static curve describes
carrier mixing. Consider an uncoupled MPR population (Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpr}, membrane
time set to one), with constant drive $Q$ and a weak added current $a\cos\omega t$.
To second order in $a$, the carrier-averaged rate shift is $a^2C(Q,\omega)$, where
\begin{equation}
C(Q,\omega)=\frac{r^*\,[\omega^2+3d^2-\nu^2]}{(d^2+\nu^2)\{[d^2+\nu^2-\omega^2]^2+4d^2\omega^2\}},
\qquad r^*=F(Q),\quad d=\frac{\Delta}{\pi r^*},\quad \nu=2\pi r^*.
\label{eq:crossover}
\end{equation}
The fixed-point eigenvalues are $-d\pm i\nu$: $d$ is the relaxation rate and $\nu$
the ringing angular frequency, both in membrane-time units. The limiting responses are
\begin{equation}
C(Q,\omega)\xrightarrow[\ \omega\to0\ ]{}\tfrac14F''(Q),\qquad
C(Q,\omega)\xrightarrow[\ \omega\gg d,\nu\ ]{}\frac{F'(Q)}{2\omega^2}.
\label{eq:crossover_limits}
\end{equation}
In the first limit the population tracks its static curve, whose curvature rectifies
the input. In the second, the voltage excursion is approximately $a/\omega$; its
square supplies a slow drive that the population reads through $F'$. Above the
inflection of $F$, these limits have opposite signs and the coefficient vanishes at
$\omega_z=\sqrt{\nu^2-3d^2}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover}a). The $300$~Hz and kHz carriers
are on the fast side for the parameters of the pyramidal--interneuron network gamma
(PING) circuit used below. That classification is
parameter-dependent: a shorter effective membrane time can bring the same physical
carrier into the adiabatic regime.

Membrane access is already included in this calculation. The linear voltage response
is $H_v(\omega)=(i\omega+d)/[(i\omega+d)^2+\nu^2]$. Dividing by its squared magnitude
gives the mixing coefficient at fixed voltage excursion,
\begin{equation}
K(\omega)=\frac{C(Q,\omega)}{|H_v(\omega)|^2}
=\frac{F'(Q)}{2}\;\frac{\omega^2+3d^2-\nu^2}{\omega^2+d^2}.
\label{eq:kfixed}
\end{equation}
This coefficient tends to $F'/2$ at high frequency. The $1/\omega^2$ suppression
at fixed current comes from $|H_v|^2$, the membrane filtering already represented
by Eq.~\eqref{eq:doors}; it is not an additional detector filter. A static curve
placed after the same membrane has the same leading carrier roll-off, although
its mixing coefficient and sign can differ. The steady-state curvature is
$F''=4r^*(3d^2-\nu^2)/(d^2+\nu^2)^3$, so the inflection occurs at $\nu^2=3d^2$.

For the full eight-state PING (Eq.~\eqref{eq:nmm2-ping}), each high-frequency current
tone of amplitude $a_j$ produces a voltage phasor $a_j/(i\omega_j\tau_E)$.
The $v_E^2$ term therefore supplies a difference-frequency source of magnitude
$a_1a_2/(\tau_E^2\omega_1\omega_2)$ inside the equation for $\tau_E\dot v_E$.
After division by $\tau_E$, this source is propagated through the network
susceptibility at $\Omega$. At four stable operating points, the resulting
response agrees with the full second-order calculation to within $1.1\%$ at
$300$~Hz, $0.1\%$ at $1$~kHz, and $0.03\%$ at $2$~kHz
(Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover}b).

\DIFaddend \subsection{Transduction class and its spatial and spectral signatures}\label{sec:transduction}
A neural response proportional to the squared envelope is not equivalent to one
proportional to the envelope. A detector implements some transduction $F(A)$ of the local
envelope $A(t)$, and the two laws predict different amplitude scaling, harmonic content and
spatial weighting.

For a narrowband field $s(t)=A(t)\cos\omega_c t$ passing through a memoryless nonlinearity
$g$, the low-frequency (baseband) output $Y_{\rm LF}$ is the average of $g$ over one
carrier cycle:
\[
Y_{\rm LF}(A)=(2\pi)^{-1}\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!g(v^*+A\cos\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
\]
Odd powers of $A$ average away, leaving only even ones:
\begin{equation}
Y_{\rm LF}(A)=g(v^*)+\tfrac14 g''(v^*)A^2+\tfrac1{64}g^{(4)}(v^*)A^4+\cdots
\label{eq:cycavg}
\end{equation}
A smooth memoryless response with $g''(v^*)\neq0$ therefore transduces $A^2$ at
leading weak-input order, whatever its detailed shape. This includes the sigmoid
mechanism of \S\ref{sec:coupling}. Recovering $A$ itself requires a qualitatively
different detector: strong rectification. A full-wave rectifier gives
$\mathrm{LPF}[\,|A\cos\omega_c t|\,]=(2/\pi)A$ and a half-wave rectifier
$(1/\pi)A$, both linear in the envelope, as does a diode-and-capacitor peak detector.
A hard threshold is not one of them: a step detector with offset $\delta$ transduces
$\pi^{-1}\arccos(\delta/A)$ for $A>\delta$ and nothing below it, which is neither linear
nor quadratic in $A$ (App.~\ref{app:bound}). Asymmetric channel rectification, threshold
crossing and release events are candidate biological realizations of rectifiers of one
kind or another. Under a linear downstream response and fixed coupling, the amplitude
sweep below distinguishes a quadratic effective drive from one proportional to the
envelope; the derivation of \S\ref{sec:coupling} assumes a smooth transfer, and this
section asks what would show that assumption to fail.

The two classes are distinguishable at a single site. At a matched target,
$A(t)=2\varepsilon|\cos(\Omega t/2)|$, whose Fourier series
$|\cos u|=\tfrac2\pi+\tfrac4\pi\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{4n^2-1}\cos 2nu$ gives a
harmonic ladder in ratios $A_{2\Omega}/A_\Omega=1/5$, $A_{3\Omega}/A_\Omega=3/35$ and
$A_{4\Omega}/A_\Omega=1/21$. The square-law output $\half A^2=\varepsilon^2(1+\cos\Omega
t)$ contains only DC and $\Omega$. For a mixed detector $F(A)=c_1A+c_2A^2$ the
pre-network ratio is
\begin{equation}

=\frac{1/5}{1+\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\,c_2\varepsilon/c_1},
\label{eq:rdet}
\end{equation}
so measuring $r_{\rm det}$ estimates $c_2\varepsilon/c_1$ directly.

Beyond harmonics and amplitude scaling, the transduction class fixes the spatial map.

$\mathcal{M}[y]\equiv\max_t y-\min_t y$ of a periodic quantity $y(t)$. The conventional TI envelope map is
$\mathcal{M}[A]=2\min(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)$, whereas the square-law detector
responds to $\mathcal{M}[\half A^2]=2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2$, so the two are related

by the dominant local polarization. It therefore does not preserve conventional TI
focality. In typical montages, where the dominant carrier is substantially larger
superficially than at depth, this reweighting degrades deep-to-superficial contrast: at
$(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)=(10,1)$ superficially against $(0.5,0.5)$ at a matched
deep target, $\mathcal{M}[A]$ favors the superficial point by $2\times$ and
$\mathcal{M}[\half A^2]$ by $40\times$. The magnitude of the degradation is not fixed by
this algebra and must be evaluated from the actual vector field distribution; a
sufficiently weak second carrier can suppress the superficial product instead. The
product law is not an account of TI focality.

driven populations amplifies it. Resonance does not override an unfavorable field
product, but it can compensate for one. This is the radio analogy taken literally:
received signal strength times receiver tuning.

Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial} is written for scalar projected fields. In general the
coupling is vectorial and compartment-specific,
$\varepsilon_i(\mathbf{x})=\boldsymbol{\lambda}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{E}_i(\mathbf{x})$, so the
mixing product $(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{E}_1)
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\!\cdot\!\mathbf{E}_2)$ is signed: its magnitude follows the product
of the projected amplitudes, while its phase inverts with either the sign of $\Sig''$ or
the relative sign of the two projections. The derivations below take the collinear,
scalar case.

\subsection{Amplification: the synaptic low-pass and the network resonance}\label{sec:carrier}
Synaptic filtering suppresses the carrier after the nonlinearity has generated
the beat. Each JR excitatory synapse has transfer function
$H_s(\omega)=Aa/(i\omega+a)^2$, a second-order low-pass with characteristic
frequency $a/(2\pi)\approx16$~Hz. It passes a $10$~Hz component with relative
amplitude about $0.7$, while reducing a $100$~Hz component to about $2.5\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:carrier}). Provided the carrier does not itself excite a responsive
mode, the loop therefore reads predominantly the generated low-frequency drive.


% fig:carrier moved to Results (Theory is equations-only)

What turns this recovered beat component into a large, frequency-selective response is
the network. Linearizing the mass about its fixed point gives a leading eigenvalue pair
$\mu_\pm=-\gamma\pm i\omega_0$; near the Hopf the response is dominated by this critical
mode, with damping $\gamma$ and modal frequency $\omega_0$.
Below a supercritical Hopf, $\gamma\to0^+$ and
the mass is a sharply tuned (high quality-factor $\mathcal{Q}=\omega_0/2\gamma$) resonator;
driving it with the demodulated beat component
\eqref{eq:demod} at $\Omega$ gives a resonant response. A constant-numerator
two-pole approximation gives
\begin{equation}
G(\Omega)\propto\big[(\omega_0^2+\gamma^2-\Omega^2)^2+(2\gamma\Omega)^2\big]^{-1/2},
\qquad G(\omega_0)\propto\frac{1}{2\gamma\omega_0}
\quad(\gamma\ll\omega_0),
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
so the response $\sim A_\Omega\,G(\Omega)$ peaks near $\omega_0$, with an
approximately Lorentzian power profile. Here $\omega_0$ is the imaginary part of
the eigenvalue; the canonical oscillator's undamped frequency is
$\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}$. Its peak is at $\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$ when
$\omega_0>\gamma$. The full network transfer also contains frequency-dependent
input and output projections, so this expression does not fix its exact peak.
The leading $1/\gamma$ growth holds near a simple Hopf with nonzero projections,
within the weak-drive regime. The rectified mean shift must also leave the operating
state on the stable side of the bifurcation. Above the Hopf an autonomous limit cycle appears,
and the demodulated drive can entrain it within an Arnold tongue; a large lock-in
amplitude alone does not establish that phase capture.

Collecting the stages, the response at the beat is
\begin{equation}
R_\Omega\simeq G(\Omega;\gamma)\;D\;\lambda_f(\omega_1)E_1\,\lambda_f(\omega_2)E_2 ,
\label{eq:receiver}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_f$ maps field to local carrier polarization, $D$ converts the
product of those polarizations into a beat drive, and $G$ maps that drive to the
observed response. Their normalization depends on where the generated drive enters.
For JR, $D=\half\Sig''(v^*)$ and $G$ is the response to a generated rate drive.
For fast-carrier QIF in the normalized units of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mpr}, $D=1$ for
the $v^2$ source and $G$ includes its conversion through the voltage and rate
dynamics. Thus the population readout is included once. In these limits $D$ is
independent of carrier frequency at fixed polarization; across the dynamic
crossover it acquires frequency dependence. A carrier sweep and a beat sweep
therefore constrain different stages rather than a single effective gain.

The resonance does not supply the quadratic mixer. The cubic Hopf normal form
responds to an in-band drive but has no second-order difference-frequency source
(App.~\ref{app:sl}). A large resonant susceptibility alone therefore does not
establish beat generation.

\subsection{Carrier access depends on the effective membrane response}\label{sec:khz}
Carrier independence at fixed polarization does not imply independence at fixed
applied field. To quantify access, consider a first-order membrane response
$H_m(\omega)=1/(1+i\omega\tau_m)$, with effective time constant $\tau_m$.
For nearby carriers, the two polarizations acquire nearly the same attenuation.
The quadratic beat then satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{A_\Omega(f_c)}{A_\Omega^{\rm direct}}
\simeq |H_m(\omega_c)|^2
=\frac{1}{1+(\omega_c\tau_m)^2}
\sim\frac{1}{(\omega_c\tau_m)^2}
\quad(\omega_c\tau_m\gg1),
\label{eq:khz}
\end{equation}
where $A_\Omega^{\rm direct}$ is the beat generated with the same static coupling
and no access attenuation. This filter belongs either in the field-to-polarization
map or in the membrane equation that computes that polarization.

Somatic passive time constants are commonly $10$--$30$~ms; reported human pyramidal
values include approximately $12$~ms \cite{deitcher2017} and $16.5\pm3.7$~ms
\cite{eyal2016}. For the illustrative $\tau_m=16$~ms used here, the power factor
$|H_m|^2$ is about $10^{-4}$ at $1$~kHz, $2.5\times10^{-5}$ at $2$~kHz, and
$10^{-6}$ at $10$~kHz. A static nonlinearity placed after this somatic response
therefore receives strongly attenuated carriers.

Cohen et al.'s periaxonal circuit provides a reason to distinguish axonal from
somatic access \cite{cohen2020periaxonal}. A conducting submyelin space, about
$12$~nm wide and partly sealed at the paranodes, separates the voltage across
myelin from that across the axonal membrane. Their minimum fitted axonal decay
was approximately $1$~ms. This is a response to injected current in a distributed
cable, rather than a measured transfer from a TI field. The AIS is another candidate
site, with a high sodium-channel density \cite{kole2008ais}; this property supports
nonlinear sensitivity but does not fix its charging time.

A shorter effective time changes carrier transmission quantitatively. The sensitivity
case $\tau_m=0.2$~ms gives $|H_m|^2\approx0.39$ at $1$~kHz and $0.14$ at $2$~kHz
(Fig.~\ref{fig:khz}). This assumed time represents the fast-compartment hypothesis
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:doors}, or motivates a shorter membrane time in a QIF reduction;
it has not been measured under TI at the AIS or a node.
In the latter description, the approach to a static transfer is governed by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:slow_synapse}; the membrane filter must not be applied again outside
the QIF equations. A short access time improves carrier transmission without,
by itself, specifying the mixing coefficient or the remaining circuit resonance.

\subsection{Operating-point dependence}\label{sec:opp}
Because $A_\Omega\propto\Sig''(v^*)$, demodulation is governed by the curvature at
the operating point, and in particular by how far $v^*$ sits from the sigmoid
inflection (Fig.~\ref{fig:opp}). The Population Excitation--Inhibition Index (PEIX)
of \cite{ruffini2025cfc} is a related but distinct quantity: the sigmoid gain
$\Sig'(v^*)$ normalized to its maximum $\Sig'(v_0)=e_0 \rho/2$ at the inflection,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PEIX}(v^*)\equiv\frac{\Sig'(v^*)}{\Sig'(v_0)}
=\operatorname{sech}^2\!\Big[\tfrac{\rho}{2}\,(v^*-v_0)\Big]\in(0,1],
\label{eq:peix}
\end{equation}
a dimensionless read-out of where $v^*$ sits, the side given by
$\operatorname{sign}(v^*-v_0)$. PEIX thus tracks $\Sig'$ (maximal at the inflection
$v_0$), whereas the demodulation gain tracks $\Sig''$ (zero at $v_0$, peaked on the
flanks, and sign-reversing across it): the two are complementary, and PEIX peaks
where the demodulation gain vanishes.

This complementarity is a property of the memoryless detector. In the exact mean field at a fast carrier the rectified DC term
follows the slope $F'$ and is largest at the inflection (\S\ref{sec:dynamic}), so the
relation between PEIX and TI efficacy is model-dependent; PEIX remains a measurable
read-out of the operating point. In the Jansen--Rit detector, anything shifting $v^*$
(neuromodulation, attention, arousal, or pathology such as psychedelics or PV-interneuron
dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease) scales, and can flip the sign of, the TI response; in
the dynamic detector the same shifts act through $F'$ and the susceptibility, without a
sign rule. The state dependence is the falsifiable handle; the sign reversal is specific
to the memoryless model.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Single-column Jansen--Rit: integration, lock-in, and controls}
All symbols and units are collected in Table~\ref{tab:notation}
(App.~\ref{app:notation}). We integrate the single-column JR model of \S\ref{sec:nmm}
(Eqs.~\eqref{eq:jr0}, parameters in Table~\ref{tab:params}) as six first-order
ODEs. The TI field enters only the pyramidal output sigmoid, Eq.~\eqref{eq:jr0},
as $s(t)$; the read-out $v=y_1-y_2$ is \emph{not} fed the field directly, so any
power at $\Omega$ in $v$ is produced by the nonlinearity and the recurrent loop,
not by trivial pass-through. Unless stated otherwise $f_c=100$~Hz and $m=1$.

We integrate with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) scheme,
$\Delta t=2\times10^{-4}$~s (50 samples per carrier cycle at $100$~Hz; reduced to
$10^{-4}$~s for carrier sweeps up to $300$~Hz). The kHz-carrier runs of
Fig.~\ref{fig:khz_direct} use $\Delta t=2\times10^{-5}$~s for the carrier sweep to
$4$~kHz (25 samples per carrier cycle at $2$~kHz, 12.5 at $4$~kHz) and
$1.5\times10^{-5}$~s for the $2$~kHz output spectrum. Halving each step changes the
reported lock-in amplitudes by less than $1\%$. The $10$~kHz figures quoted in
\S\ref{sec:khz} are analytic evaluations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:khz}, not simulations.

The resonance sweep in Fig.~\ref{fig:res} uses $10$~s of settling and a $20$~s
measurement window. Long windows reduce transients from weakly damped modes and
average beating when autonomous activity persists. Other runs use the windows
specified in their scripts.
The two-tone $2$~kHz example (Fig.~\ref{fig:khz_twotone}) uses $p=400$~s$^{-1}$,
$C=135$, and $0.5$~mV per tone with $\tau=0.2$~ms. From a zero initial state,
it settles for $4$~s and records $4$~s at $\Delta t=1.5\times10^{-5}$~s,
sampling before each RK4 step. Its amplitude spectra use plain-mean subtraction
and a Hann window. Parameter
sweeps are vectorized: the state is an
$(N,6)$ array advanced simultaneously for all $N$ parameter combinations, so a full
resonance map runs in seconds.

The full bifurcation diagram (Fig.~\ref{fig:bif}) is computed by numerical continuation of
the fixed-point and limit-cycle branches. We independently locate the operating
supercritical Hopf by solving, for each $p$, the self-consistent scalar equation for the
resting fixed point $v=y_1-y_2$, forming the $6\times6$ Jacobian numerically and
diagonalizing it: the Hopf is the $p$ at which the leading eigenvalue pair crosses the
imaginary axis with $\mathrm{Im}\,\mu_+$ in the alpha band. This gives $p\approx315$~Hz
and $f_0=\mathrm{Im}\,\mu_+/2\pi\approx11.1$~Hz---consistent with the continuation and
with the JR bifurcation analysis of \cite{grimbert2006}. For $p>315$ the fixed point is a
stable focus (resonator); between the saddle-node on an invariant circle (SNIC,
$p\!\approx\!114$) and the Hopf the column free-runs on the alpha limit cycle.

\subsection{Laminar two-band model (LaNMM)}
We complement the single-column analysis with simulations of the laminar neural
mass model (LaNMM) of \S\ref{sec:nmm}. We integrate the full 28-variable column
\cite{ruffini2025cfc,sanchezTodo2023}, with the field entering as in the single
JR column, as a membrane polarization added inside the sigmoid argument of the target
population,
\begin{equation}
\varphi_X(t)=\Sig\!\big(v_X(t)+s(t)\big),\qquad X\in\{P_1,P_2\},
\label{eq:lanmm_drive}
\end{equation}
with $s(t)$ the AM or two-tone field of \S\ref{sec:coupling} (amplitude $\varepsilon$ in
mV, beat $\Omega=2\pi\Delta f$, carrier $\omega_c=2\pi f_c$) and all three external drives
held flat at their baselines (Fig.~\ref{fig:lanmm_setup}). A perturbation of an external
input \emph{rate} would require clipping at zero
once its excursion exceeds the baseline, and a hard clip is a half-wave rectifier---a
diode detector placed upstream of the sigmoid, which would inject a line at $\Delta f$
into the drive before the population nonlinearity received it. A signed membrane
polarization carries no such constraint and none is applied. Because the LaNMM and JR
sigmoids share the voltage scale $1/\rho\approx1.8$~mV, $\varepsilon$ is directly
comparable between the two models.

Integration is a vectorized fixed-step RK4
($\Delta t=10^{-4}$~s) advancing the whole parameter grid simultaneously, as in the JR
sweeps. The maps report the
alpha-band ($8$--$12$~Hz) power of the $P_1$ and $P_2$ membrane potentials as the mean
squared magnitude of the Hann-windowed fast Fourier transform (FFT) over the band, in arbitrary units, over a
post-transient window. This measure includes autonomous and driven activity and
does not isolate a phase-coherent response to stimulation.

Two sweeps probe the column: an amplitude--beat sweep over
$(\varepsilon,\Delta f)$ at fixed carrier, and a carrier--beat sweep
over $(f_c,\Delta f)$ at fixed $\varepsilon$, built on the LaNMM module of
\cite{ruffini2025cfc}. The maps
(Figs.~\ref{fig:lanmm_p2},~\ref{fig:lanmm_p1}) show the \emph{signed} stimulation-induced
change, alpha power minus the unstimulated ($\varepsilon=0$) baseline, on a zero-centered
scale; over most of the $P_1$-driven grids the change is negative. For the
alpha resonance we instead drive the deep $P_1$ loop directly and sweep its input against
$\Delta f$, reading the lock-in (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}) of $v_{P_1}$ at $\Delta f$.

The additional carrier comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:lanmm} uses the lock-in
of $v_{P_1}$, averaged at each carrier over 17 beats: $7$--$9$ and
$12.25$--$14$~Hz in $0.25$~Hz steps. These lie more than $1.5$~Hz from the
field-free alpha peak near $10.6$~Hz. We denote this arithmetic mean by $S(f_c)$.
The carrier grid is $25$--$80$~Hz in $1$~Hz steps, with equal tone amplitudes
$\varepsilon=1.5$~mV for $P_2$ drive and $0.5$~mV for $P_1$ drive. After $2$~s
of settling, we compare $3$ and $10$~s measurement windows. Field-free controls
give mean background amplitudes of $0.0048$ and $0.0012$~mV over the same beats.
This background includes a persistent component near $8$~Hz, so it is not solely
finite-window leakage. The statistic compares carrier profiles within each drive
condition; the unequal amplitudes do not support a direct sensitivity comparison
between targets.

\subsection{QIF spiking network (microscale of NMM2)}\label{sec:qif-methods}
NMM2 is the exact infinite-population mean field of all-to-all QIF neurons
\cite{montbrio2015,ruffini2025rosetta,clusella2023}. We integrate that network directly
to compare beat-locked and ongoing activity at the spike level (\S\ref{sec:qif}): $N=8000$ QIF neurons per
population, parameters identical to the PING mean field (Eq.~\eqref{eq:nmm2-ping}),
with finite spike threshold $V_{\rm peak}=100$ and reset $-V_{\rm peak}$ in model
voltage units, excitabilities $\eta_j\sim$ Lorentzian$(\bar\eta,\Delta)$ sampled by deterministic
quantiles, second-order synapses driven by the empirical population rates, and a genuine
two-tone field entering the excitatory membranes. Spiking and threshold dynamics are
where the AM surrogate and the physical waveform cannot be assumed equivalent beyond
perturbation theory, so this run uses two tones throughout.

The common background drive $\bar\eta$ places the field-free mean field below or
above a gamma Hopf at $\bar\eta_H=1.110$ ($f_0=54.0$~Hz). We use
$\bar\eta=0.85$ with a $55$~Hz beat and $\bar\eta=1.5$ with a $42$~Hz beat.
Both runs have carrier $f_c=300$~Hz and amplitude $A_f=35$ per tone. These are
finite-amplitude illustrations: the fast-carrier estimate gives a voltage excursion
$A_f/(\tau_E\omega_c)\approx1.2$ per tone in the model's voltage units. The field
can therefore alter the dynamical regime, even when the unstimulated state is below
Hopf. We label the conditions by their field-free state and separate the beat-locked
component from other gamma activity. The spectra and rate folds do not by themselves
establish phase capture (\S\ref{sec:vocab}).

We also run both conditions at $A_f=10$ per tone with the same measurement
settings. For $\bar\eta=0.85$, separate checks double the population size,
double the measurement window, or halve the integration step; further runs raise
$V_{\rm peak}$ to $200$ and $400$ at both time steps. An equal-RMS
mean-field control replaces the two tones by one $300$~Hz carrier with peak
amplitude $\sqrt{2}A_f$, using the same duration: both inputs have mean-square
amplitude $A_f^2$ over these windows. The carrier comparison starts from the
field-free equilibrium and uses $800$~ms settling and $800$~ms measurement,
with a $3200$~ms window to check spectral leakage at the larger amplitude.

For two nearby carriers, the fast-carrier approximation gives a constant shift
of the excitatory drive, $\delta\eta_E\simeq A_f^2/(\omega_c\tau_E)^2$, from the
quadratic voltage term. We refine this estimate using the full PING linear carrier
responses and their second-order DC source, then solve for the carrier-averaged
equilibrium and its stability, including the source's dependence on that equilibrium.
This approximation isolates the mean shift; direct two-tone integrations retain
the beat forcing and provide a separate check.

The network is integrated with Euler steps of $0.0025$~ms, settled for $800$~ms (over
four field-free relaxation times at $\bar\eta=0.85$, where the mean-field relaxation time is
$176$~ms) and measured over an integer number of beat cycles, $44$ at $55$~Hz and $34$ at
$42$~Hz ($800$ and $810$~ms), removing the phase-coverage bias from incomplete
beat cycles. Envelope-locked timing is quantified by the quadrature lock-in $A_\Omega$ of
$r_E(t)$ at $\Delta f$, reported as the modulation depth $A_\Omega/\langle r_E\rangle$ (a
stable measure; the off$\to$on lock-in ratio would divide by the field-free floor), and
per neuron by the pairwise phase consistency of each neuron's spike phases at $\Delta f$
\cite{vinck2010}, which is unbiased in the spike count, over neurons with at least ten
spikes in the window.

In the $A_f=35$ convergence checks, halving the time step changes the lock-in and the pooled vector
strength by under $1\%$; doubling the spike-detection threshold changes the lock-in by
about $1\%$ and the mean rate by $2\%$, but the pooled vector strength at
$\bar\eta=0.85$ by about $45\%$, so phase concentration, unlike rate modulation, is sensitive to
how spike times are defined.

\subsection{Vocabulary: forced response, entrainment, and gating}
\emph{Forced response} denotes the component driven at the beat $\Delta f$,
quantified by the lock-in amplitude (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}). A purely forced regime
requires a stable operating state under stimulation; being below Hopf with the field
off is insufficient. \emph{Entrainment} is reserved for its
dynamical-systems meaning, frequency capture of a \emph{self-sustained} oscillator
\cite{pikovsky2001}; in the maps we screen for it with the dominance criterion
$|f_{\rm out}-\Delta f|<0.3$~Hz on the dominant output frequency. This identifies
frequency dominance, not phase capture. \emph{Gating} is amplitude modulation of a persisting rhythm without
frequency capture \cite{canolty2010,tort2010}. Capture and gating are dynamically different, and the same word would otherwise cover
both. The QIF rate fold measures beat-locked firing, which does not by itself
establish gating of gamma amplitude. Appendix~\ref{sec:vocab} gives the metrics
and the frequency-dominance protocol.

\section{Results}
The Jansen--Rit column and the exact next-generation mean field (NMM2) both generate a
difference-frequency component and amplify it near a Hopf bifurcation. The former uses
a memoryless sigmoid; the latter derives the nonlinearity from QIF dynamics. Their
detector coefficients differ, but both separate beat generation from recurrent gain.
Coupling controls that gain at fixed generated drive in the JR column
(\S\ref{sec:jcurve}), and resonance amplifies beat-locked modulation more strongly than
the mean rate (\S\ref{sec:timing}). Finite-amplitude QIF simulations then show how a
beat-locked component coexists with gamma activity at the spike level. The laminar
two-band column extends the analysis to carriers of $25$--$80$~Hz and drives up to
$3$~mV (App.~\ref{app:lanmm}).

\subsection{What the mesoscale adds: coupling sets the gain at fixed detection}\label{sec:jcurve}
The resonance sweeps above drove the column toward the Hopf with the external input
$p$, but $p$ also shifts the operating point, entangling detection ($\Sig''$) with
amplification. To isolate amplification, we vary the JR global connectivity $C$ (coupling
strength $J$) and solve analytically for the drive $p(C)$ that
\emph{pins} the field-free operating point $v^*$, so the detection gain $\Sig''(v^*)$ and the
open-loop detector amplitude $A_{\rm open}\simeq\half|\Sig''(v^*)|\varepsilon^2 m$ are held
constant. As $C\!\to\!C^*$ (the Hopf, $C^*\!\approx\!137$) the damping
$\gamma\!\to\!0$, the demodulated response grows $\sim30\times$ and the envelope
resonance sharpens (Fig.~\ref{fig:jcurve}a,b), a network effect since
the local detector curvature is fixed while the recurrent transfer changes. Its modal frequency and
input--output projections vary with $C$ as well as its damping. The rate-to-voltage gain
$A_\Omega/A_{\rm open}$ (mV\,s) tracks $1/\gamma$ before saturating near criticality
(Fig.~\ref{fig:jcurve}c); driving the same column to the Hopf through the input $p$
rather than the coupling $C$ gives a similar leading gain--$\gamma$ trend.
Both paths approach a weakly damped mode; their finite-distance gains need not
coincide because the modal projections can differ.

The direct low-frequency control tests whether this amplification favors TI.
For the AM surrogate with amplitude $\varepsilon_{\rm TI}$ and modulation depth
$m$, and a tACS input of amplitude $\varepsilon_{\rm tACS}$, the ratio of voltage
lock-in amplitudes in linear response is
\[
\mathcal R_{\rm lin}
=\frac{|\Sig''(v^*)|\,\varepsilon_{\rm TI}^2m}{2\Sig'(v^*)\,\varepsilon_{\rm tACS}}.
\]
The common network transfer cancels, so the ratio has no dependence on $\gamma$
when both responses are linear about the same state. At the amplitudes used in
Figs.~\ref{fig:jcurve} and~\ref{fig:tacs}, $\mathcal R_{\rm lin}=0.143$.
The cached sweeps give approximately $0.14$ far from the Hopf, rising to $0.24$
at $\gamma=1.07$~s$^{-1}$ and $0.47$ at $0.12$~s$^{-1}$.
These are response ratios for the stated unequal input amplitudes, not an
equal-power comparison. In controls at three couplings, matching the tACS input
to TI's leading beat source gives TI/tACS output ratios of $0.99$, $0.94$, and
$0.92$. Increasing that direct input by a factor of $6.99$ increases its response
by $6.97$, $3.95$, and $1.96$, respectively. These controls support earlier
saturation of the larger response, accompanied by stimulation-induced mean
shifts that field-free pinning does not cancel. The linear cancellation therefore
does not extend to arbitrary finite-amplitude responses near criticality.

\subsection{The resonance amplifies the envelope-locked modulation more than the mean rate}\label{sec:timing}
The demodulator feeds the network a constant term and a line at the beat $\Omega$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:taylor}), and the transfer $G(\Omega)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gain} treats them
differently: $G(\omega_0)\propto1/(2\gamma\omega_0)$ diverges as the Hopf is approached,
while $G(0)\propto1/(\omega_0^2+\gamma^2)$ stays finite. Pinning $v^*$ to hold the local detector
curvature fixed, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:jcurve}, and driving the column toward the Hopf
through the coupling, we read both quantities from the same observable, the pyramidal
rate $r_P=\Sig(v+s)$ with the field in its argument. The beat-locked modulation of $r_P$ at
$\Delta f=f_0$ grows $13.5\times$ ($26$ to $346$~mHz), tracking $1/\gamma$ before
saturating near criticality; the shift of its mean grows too, $5.8\times$ ($-1.3$ to
$-7.7$~mHz), through rectification of the growing oscillation, but stays below $0.2\%$ of
the $3.9$~Hz baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:timing}). Modulation exceeds mean shift by $19\times$
far from the Hopf and by $45$--$66\times$ near it. The network amplifies the
envelope-locked modulation far more than the mean rate, so a small mean-rate change is
the expected signature even where TI is acting, the mesoscopic counterpart of the
observation that TI alters spike timing more than firing rate in the primate brain
\cite{vieira2024}. The mean shift is small but nonzero, so the comparison must use
commensurate measures, as here.

\subsection{QIF responses distinguish beat modulation from field-induced gamma}\label{sec:qif}
The QIF network connects the population description to individual spikes
\cite{montbrio2015,ruffini2025rosetta,clusella2023}. With $8000$ neurons per population
and the same parameters as the PING mean field, it distinguishes a forced beat
response from a stimulation-induced change of dynamical regime
(Figs.~\ref{fig:qif_raster},~\ref{fig:qif_regimes}).

At $\bar\eta=0.85$ and tone amplitude $A_f=10$, the spiking network has a beat
depth of $0.093$ at $55$~Hz and a mean-rate increase of $2.8\%$: modulation exceeds
the fractional mean-rate change by a factor of $3.4$. The mean field gives a
larger depth, $0.128$. Raising the spike cutoff from $V_{\rm peak}=100$ to
$200$ and $400$ gives depths $0.127$ and $0.139$, while the mean-rate increase
stays at $2.8$--$2.9\%$. At the original cutoff, doubling the population gives
$0.083$ and halving the time step gives $0.093$. The response is therefore
sensitive to the finite spike cutoff. Halving the step at cutoffs $200$ and
$400$ gives depths $0.121$ and $0.141$, respectively: cutoff dependence persists,
and quantitative convergence remains unestablished at this population size.
At the plotted cutoff, the pooled spike-phase vector strength is $0.046$;
median per-neuron phase consistency remains negative in field-off and field-on
windows at all three cutoffs. At
$\bar\eta=1.5$, the corresponding $42$~Hz depth is smaller, $0.023$, against a
$1.6\%$ mean-rate increase, and the existing gamma dominates the raster.

At the larger amplitude $A_f=35$ and $\bar\eta=0.85$, stimulation produces
a dominant line near $52$~Hz, with modulation depth about $0.70$, and a smaller component
at the $55$~Hz beat, with depth $0.29$. The second-order carrier-averaged system
predicts an excitatory drive shift $\delta\eta_E\approx1.65$, beyond the E-only
Hopf threshold $\delta\eta_E^*\approx0.286$, with a leading eigenvalue whose real
part is $+0.032$~ms$^{-1}$. Direct mean-field integration reproduces the off-beat
line, supporting the interpretation of panel~(b) as field-induced gamma with a
smaller forced component.

The equal-RMS unmodulated carrier provides a control for this distinction in
the mean field (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_regimes}c,d). It produces a rectified E-drive
shift within $3\%$ of TI's and a similar mean-rate increase. At $A_f=35$, both
waveforms induce gamma near $51$~Hz, whereas TI also produces the $55$~Hz beat
component. Increasing the measurement window from $0.8$ to $3.2$~s reduces
the carrier's apparent $55$~Hz depth from $0.011$ to $9.2\times10^{-5}$,
consistent with spectral leakage, while TI's beat depth remains $0.30$--$0.31$.
At $A_f=10$, neither induces gamma, and TI alone produces a resolved beat
response. The control separates induction of an oscillation from its
beat-frequency response; it has not been run in the spiking network.

At $\bar\eta=1.5$, gamma activity is already present without stimulation. With the
field on, the rhythm near $53$--$54$~Hz remains about four times larger than the
$42$~Hz beat-locked component. Gamma therefore dominates the raster in panel~(d);
the coexistence of the rhythms produces slower variations in burst height. Folding the
rate on the beat phase suppresses the non-beat-locked gamma and exposes the smaller
component. The inset shows that phase average, not the full rate variation.

The population spectrum also contains a sum sideband near $95$~Hz and a smaller
difference component near $11$~Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}b). These lines establish
nonlinear mixing, without uniquely identifying gamma-amplitude gating or phase
modulation. The field-free gamma spectra of the spiking network and exact mean field
agree in frequency (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}a).

At $A_f=35$, the mean rate rises by about $8$--$11\%$ across the two conditions, while the beat modulation
depth, $A_\Omega/\langle r_E\rangle$, rises from below $0.003$ to $0.29$
($\bar\eta=0.85$) and $0.23$ ($\bar\eta=1.5$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}c), measured over $44$ and $34$ whole beat
cycles after $800$~ms of settling (Methods). Timing modulation therefore exceeds the
relative mean-rate change by a factor of about three at the population level. Per neuron
the locking is weak: the pairwise phase consistency of single-neuron spike phases at
$\Delta f$ rises from its field-free control (median $-0.02$) to a median of
$0.003$--$0.005$, and the pooled vector strength from below $0.02$ to $0.12$--$0.13$. The
population's beat-locked component is built from many weakly biased cells. This gives a
qualitative counterpart of the timing-versus-rate distinction in \S\ref{sec:timing}
and in primate recordings \cite{vieira2024}; the present amplitudes do not establish
the size of that effect under weak physiological stimulation.

\subsection{Falsifiable predictions and tests}\label{sec:predictions}
The mechanism makes four predictions, each with an experimental handle.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Carrier dependence separates access from mixing.} In the static limit
and in the fast-carrier QIF asymptote, the leading beat is independent of $f_c$ at
fixed local polarization, provided the carrier does not excite another network mode.
At fixed applied field, access restores frequency dependence
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:khz}); detector dynamics can add it near the crossover.
\emph{Test:} measure or estimate local polarization during a carrier sweep and
compare the response before and after correcting for access. The remaining
frequency dependence constrains the mixing mechanism. Its cutoff is to be
measured, not assigned to a particular compartment in advance.
\item \textbf{State- and carrier-dependent response.} The generated beat depends on
the operating point through the detector coefficient, and the two modeled detectors
disagree on how: the memoryless sigmoid follows $\Sig''(v^*)$ and reverses sign across the
inflection, where PEIX (Eq.~\eqref{eq:peix}) peaks; the dynamic mean field at a fast
carrier mixes in the voltage dynamics and is read through the rate and network
susceptibility; its DC rectification follows $F'$, without the same curvature sign rule
(\S\ref{sec:dynamic}). \emph{Test:} vary tonic drive and carrier frequency while
measuring both amplitude and phase of the beat response. Compare these with the
predicted mixing coefficient after accounting for changes in network susceptibility;
a response-amplitude change alone does not isolate the detector.
\item \textbf{Transduction class.} A smooth memoryless response with nonzero curvature
transduces $A^2$ at weak input, whereas an ideal envelope follower transduces $A$
(\S\ref{sec:transduction}), and the two are separable at a
single matched target. The former gives $Y_\Omega\propto\varepsilon^2$ with a second-harmonic
ratio vanishing as $\varepsilon\!\to\!0$; the latter gives $Y_\Omega\propto\varepsilon$ with
the ratio pinned at $1/5$. \emph{Test:} sweep field amplitude off resonance (so that both
lines sit on the flat part of $G$) and read the log--log slope together with
$Y_{2\Omega}/Y_\Omega$; on resonance the network suppresses the second harmonic by
$\sim3\mathcal{Q}$ and the test must be corrected by an independently measured
$G(2\Omega)/G(\Omega)$.

\item \textbf{Frequency selectivity and state-dependence.} The response is largest
when $\Delta f$ matches a region's resonant rhythm and is amplified as $1/\gamma$ near
a bifurcation. Changes in operating state can therefore alter both the generated
drive and the recurrent gain \cite{ruffini2025cfc}. \emph{Test:}
sweep $\Delta f$ through a region's intrinsic band across brain states and track the
resonant peak and its width.
\end{enumerate}

Its accompanying DC source follows the summed carrier intensities under the
conditions of \S\ref{sec:transduction}, providing an operating-point shift that
the equal-RMS carrier control isolates and that can cross the population Hopf
(\S\ref{sec:qif})}\DIFaddend .
Orientation, access, operating point, and network susceptibility further shape
the response. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{For a smooth memoryless transfer, carrier averaging is even in the
envelope amplitude, giving quadratic transduction when the second-order coefficient
is nonzero. Dynamic nonlinearities can also mix at second order, with
frequency-dependent coefficients. }\DIFdelend A sharp rectifier can \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{instead }\DIFdelend approximate
envelope following over a finite amplitude range; smoothing or heterogeneity
can restore quadratic behavior at smaller inputs. The amplitude and harmonic
tests in \S\ref{sec:transduction} distinguish these regimes.

Stimulation intensity can also change the mechanism. At high intensities, TI and
unmodulated kHz fields can have similar excitation thresholds, with modulation
patterning firing rather than lowering the threshold
\cite{opancar2025,semenov2025}; some peripheral responses do not require envelope
extraction \cite{peripheral2023}. The weak-input calculation here concerns
modulation of ongoing activity through mixing and amplification
\cite{vieira2024,missey2026}. Direct carrier-evoked spiking and conduction block
are outside its scope. The finite-amplitude QIF examples illustrate a related
distinction: rectification can shift a stable population across its Hopf and induce
gamma activity alongside the beat-locked component. The driven component must be
separated from that change in regime. The boundary depends on the initial state's
distance from the bifurcation as well as on field amplitude. These examples and the laminar extension describe
behavior beyond the weak-input expansion; they do not establish physiological thresholds.

The recurrent amplifier is shared with direct low-frequency stimulation. In our
coupling sweep, holding the JR field-free operating point fixed holds the local curvature
fixed while the network gain changes. A direct in-band drive shows the same
$1/\gamma$ growth and resonance sharpening (Fig.~\ref{fig:tacs}), consistent with
the known resonant susceptibility of recurrent excitatory--inhibitory networks
\cite{ledoux2011,clusella2023}. Near the Hopf, this is temporal critical slowing
in a mean-field model, not evidence of a diverging spatial correlation length.
Related models already reproduce tACS entrainment and AM-driven phase locking
\cite{ali2013,negahbani2018,krause2023}. The separation here is between the drive
generated by mixing and the response to that drive. When TI and tACS are matched
for effective in-band input, observable, and operating state, they share the
linear amplifier. Within linear response, resonance alone therefore gives TI
no efficiency advantage. At finite amplitude the response ratio can change near
the Hopf, as the coupling sweep demonstrates (\S\ref{sec:jcurve}); its value then
depends on drive amplitude and the driven state as well as on field-free damping.

Absolute response estimates remain sensitive to access and transduction.
Measured static field-to-voltage couplings are $0.12$--$0.21$~mV per V/m in
hippocampus \cite{bikson2004,deans2007} and reach about $0.5$~mV per V/m in cortical
L5/6 pyramidal cells \cite{radman2009}; human cortical fields near $0.8$~V/m have
been reported at $2$~mA \cite{huang2017,huang2017corr}. These static couplings do
not fix the coupling at kHz. For equal TI carrier polarizations $\varepsilon$,
the JR beat drive is $\Sig''\varepsilon^2/2$, whereas a direct drive of the same
peak polarization gives $2\Sig'\varepsilon$. Their leading amplitude ratio is
$\eta_{\rm det}\simeq|\Sig''|\varepsilon/(4\Sig')$: the relative efficiency decreases
as the input is weakened in this perturbative regime. A larger recurrent gain
amplifies both drives.

For scale, a reverberation time $\tau_{\rm rev}=1/\gamma$ gives a near-Hopf
quality factor $\mathcal Q\simeq\pi f_0\tau_{\rm rev}$ and power bandwidth
approximately $1/(\pi\tau_{\rm rev})$. Population relaxation over a few hundred
milliseconds \cite{wilting2018} would give a quality factor of order ten at
$10$~Hz and a bandwidth of order $1$~Hz. This is an order-of-magnitude comparison:
the measured relaxation is not specific to the alpha mode. Narrow amplification
also makes detuning consequential.

Primate measurements illustrate both the relevance and the limits of an efficiency
comparison. Vieira et al.\ \cite{vieira2024} used modulation depths of
$0.5$--$0.62$~V/m and reported TI effects at $0.34$ times those of conventional
tACS (95\% confidence interval $0.04$--$0.65$). Their within-electrode AM comparison
compensated for frequency-dependent current shunting and matched extracellular
modulation depth near the recorded cells. A favorable fast-access estimate with
$\varepsilon\approx0.1$~mV gives only about $0.8\%$ for the JR detector, below the
reported ratio. This discrepancy is not a calibrated rejection of that detector:
the comparison involved twenty neurons, with ten responding to at least one
modality and seven to both; phase-locking value (PLV) depends on baseline locking
and network state; and the matched extracellular fields do not ensure matched
polarization at the cellular mixer. An unspecified nonlinear relation between
generated drive and PLV also prevents identification of the input-amplitude
exponent. The data establish a weaker AM effect than the matched-depth
low-frequency drive, but do not isolate the transduction law.

For comparison, the matched-peak efficiency over the logistic family has a supremum
of approximately $0.348$ (App.~\ref{app:bound}). Approaching it at weak fields
requires a transfer curve narrowed to microvolt scales; the limiting detector is
step-like and has a second-harmonic ratio near $0.15$, distinct from both weak
square-law detection and linear envelope following. This bound applies to that
memoryless family and matching convention, not to the dynamic QIF detector or to
all cellular mechanisms. Its proximity to an experimental ratio is not a fit.

Quantitative physiological predictions require calibration of access and mixing.
Their values are not fixed by the quadratic scaling law. In comparisons of
stimulus-evoked firing, Hodgkin--Huxley and Frankenhaeuser--Huxley models reproduce
responses missed by the tested leaky, exponential, and adaptive-exponential
integrate-and-fire models \cite{plovie2025}. Such model dependence concerns the
specific response, without invalidating the weak-input expansion. Noise,
heterogeneity beyond the assumed excitability distribution, and interactions
between columns can broaden or shift the deterministic resonances. Cascading
ordinary synaptic filters cannot solve carrier access, because they attenuate
the carrier before a subsequent nonlinearity receives it. Human results are also
mixed, including reported peripheral effects without central effects
\cite{iszak2023} and null occipito-parietal alpha-power modulation
\cite{mansourinezhad2025}. Testing the proposed mechanism requires measuring
access, generated beat, and network tuning in the same preparation.

\section{The cubic Hopf normal form lacks a quadratic mixer}\label{app:sl}
The Stuart--Landau equation \cite{pikovsky2001}
\begin{equation}
\dot z=(\alpha+i\omega_0)z-|z|^2z+\mathcal F(t),\qquad
\gamma=-\alpha>0,
\label{eq:sl}
\end{equation}
describes a complex mode $z$ with damping $\gamma$ and natural angular frequency
$\omega_0$, driven by the applied input $\mathcal F$. For a weak input
$\mathcal F=\varepsilon f(t)$ on the stable side, the perturbative response has
the form $z=\varepsilon z_1+\varepsilon^3z_3+\cdots$. There is no second-order
source: the first-order response contains only the applied frequencies, and the
cubic first contributes at order $\varepsilon^3$. Thus the normal form has a
resonant linear susceptibility but lacks the quadratic difference-frequency
generation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:demod}. This statement concerns the weak-input
expansion; it is not an exclusion of every higher-order intermodulation or
strong-drive response.

In the numerical control ($\alpha=-0.5$, $f_0=10$~Hz, $f_c=100$~Hz,
$\varepsilon=0.5$), a square-law detector gives a beat-frequency lock-in amplitude
of $2.5\times10^{-1}$, while $\mathrm{Re}\,z$ gives approximately
$2\times10^{-8}$, at the numerical floor. Driving the same mode directly in band
recovers the forced $1/\gamma$ growth. The control separates resonant amplification
from the nonlinear source required to drive it at the beat.

\section{Efficiency of a memoryless transfer against a direct in-band drive}\label{app:bound}
For the logistic family of memoryless transfers, compare balanced TI tones of
polarization amplitude $\varepsilon$ each with a direct in-band drive of amplitude
$2\varepsilon$, matching peak polarization. Their leading drive amplitudes are
$|\Sig''|\varepsilon^2/2$ and $2\Sig'\varepsilon$, so
$\eta_{\rm det}\simeq|\Sig''|\varepsilon/(4\Sig')$.
This ratio concerns the specified transfer family and input matching, not TI in
general or the dynamic detector of \S\ref{sec:dynamic}.

Writing $s=\Sig/(2e_0)\in(0,1)$ gives
$\Sig'=2e_0\rho s(1-s)$ and
$\Sig''=2e_0\rho^2s(1-s)(1-2s)$. Since
$\mathrm{PEIX}=4s(1-s)$, the leading ratio satisfies
\begin{equation}
\eta_{\rm det}\simeq\frac{\rho\,\varepsilon}{4}\sqrt{1-\mathrm{PEIX}}
\;\le\;\frac{\rho\,\varepsilon}{4}.
\label{eq:etabound}
\end{equation}
It vanishes at the inflection, where the quadratic coefficient is zero, and
approaches its bound in the saturated tails. This maximizes a ratio rather than
an absolute response: both drives vanish in those tails. For the JR slope
$\rho=0.56$~mV$^{-1}$, the perturbative bound is $1.4\%$ at
$\varepsilon=0.1$~mV and $0.28\%$ at $\varepsilon=0.02$~mV.

The slope is itself a parameter, so the question is what the whole sigmoid family can do.
The logistic transfer depends on voltage only through $\rho(v-v_0)$, so writing
\begin{equation}
u=\rho\,\varepsilon,\qquad x=\rho\,(v^*-v_0),
\label{eq:reduced}
\end{equation}
we have $\Sig(v^*+\varepsilon q(t))=2e_0\,L(x+u\,q(t))$ for any normalized waveform $q$,
with $L(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1}$. Every Fourier amplitude, and hence their ratio, is a function
of $(u,x)$ alone; the factor $2e_0$ cancels. Maximizing over $v^*$ and $\rho$ at fixed
$\varepsilon$ is therefore the same as maximizing over $(u,x)$, and the family supremum is
independent of field strength. Evaluating the exact cycle response rather than its
quadratic truncation and optimizing $x$ at each $u$, the ratio rises monotonically over the
range explored and converges to the hard-threshold limit
\begin{equation}
\sup_{\rho,\,v^*}\eta_{\rm det}=0.3479 ,
\label{eq:etasup}
\end{equation}
approached as $\rho\to\infty$ at a threshold offset $\delta$ (the distance from the
operating point to the inflection) with $\delta/2\varepsilon=0.523$. There is no
finite optimal transfer width: narrower is always better, and reaching $95\%$ of the ceiling
requires $u\gtrsim6$, that is $1/\rho\lesssim\varepsilon/6$.

Such a detector is not weak. At the optimum,
and at the Vieira-scale polarization $\varepsilon\!\simeq\!0.1$~mV, its open-loop beat drive
exceeds the physiological-slope sigmoid's by some three orders of magnitude for the same
$e_0$ and waveform, because the field excursion is about twice the threshold offset and the
transfer switches across its full range instead of responding linearly. The disparity grows
as the field weakens, the smooth sigmoid's response falling as $\varepsilon^2$ while a
threshold repositioned with the field keeps its excursion.

The obstacle is the width.
At Vieira et al.'s fields the ceiling is approached only for $1/\rho\lesssim17\;\mu$V, and
at human polarizations for $1/\rho\lesssim3\;\mu$V, with the threshold offset held to the
same order as the applied polarization. The width of a neural-mass sigmoid is set by the
spread of thresholds and noise across $\sim10^4$ neurons and is of order millivolts. A
microvolt width lies far below that and reads as an idealized microscopic threshold or
rectifier rather than as a neural-mass sigmoid; real thresholds are themselves noisy and
state-dependent, so it is not a measured single-cell property either. The
regime in which the family approaches its ceiling is thus one in which the ``population
sigmoid'' has ceased to describe a population.

The limit is a step detector, not an envelope follower. For a unit step at offset
$\delta$ the carrier-averaged transduction is $\pi^{-1}\arccos(\delta/A)$ for $A>\delta$
and zero below; with $\beta=\delta/2\varepsilon$ its tACS fundamental is
$(2/\pi)\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$ and its TI coefficient reduces by parts to
$\eta_{\rm det}=2\beta\,[K(m)-E(m)]/(\pi\sqrt{1-\beta^2})$ with $m=1-\beta^2$ and $K,E$
the complete elliptic integrals, giving $0.3479$ at $\beta=0.523$ by direct quadrature and
by the elliptic form alike (\texttt{threshold\_bound\_check.py}). At that optimum the
step's second-to-first harmonic magnitude ratio is $0.151$, not the $1/5$ of a linear
envelope follower. A bounded nondecreasing transfer is a positive mixture of shifted steps
and both Fourier coefficients are linear in the transfer, so the step optimum bounds the
family; the maximum itself is a numerical evaluation, not a certified bound.

\section{Vocabulary and metrics}\label{sec:vocab}
\emph{Forced response.} Below the Hopf the column does not oscillate on its own;
we quantify its response by the lock-in amplitude at the beat $\Delta f$
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}). There is no autonomous rhythm to entrain in the stable-focus
J-curve, timing, and tACS sweeps.

\emph{Frequency dominance and entrainment.} Above the Hopf the column free-runs
at $f_0$. A $1{:}1$ phase-capture test would follow the unwrapped phase difference
from the drive and require absence of secular drift. Our maps instead report
$|f_{\rm out}-\Delta f|<0.3$~Hz, where $f_{\rm out}$ is the largest non-DC FFT
peak. This is a frequency-dominance screen: a forced component can outgrow a
persisting autonomous one without capturing it. When the components coexist,
instantaneous-phase estimates also require their separation
\cite{lachaux1999}. The maps therefore identify a dominance region, not a
measured Arnold tongue.

\emph{Beat-locked rate and gating.} Gating means that the amplitude of a
persisting rhythm is modulated by the beat without frequency capture
\cite{canolty2010,tort2010}. The QIF statistic used here is the lock-in of the
population rate $r_E$ at $\Delta f$, complemented by the rate folded on beat
phase over whole cycles. It measures beat-locked firing. A raw rate fold does
not extract the gamma amplitude, and sidebands can arise from amplitude or phase
modulation. The above-Hopf QIF panels therefore establish coexistence and mixing
of the beat and gamma rhythms; a gamma-amplitude estimate would be needed to
identify gating specifically.

The protocol for Fig.~\ref{fig:entrain} places the column on the limit-cycle side
(input $p$ between the saddle-node on invariant circle and the Hopf), drives it
with $s(t)=\varepsilon\cos\Omega t$, and records the dominant frequency. Grid
widths and the clipping check are specified in the code. As the Hopf is approached,
the autonomous cycle shrinks and the frequency-dominance region broadens in these
simulations. Its width and the stable-focus susceptibility describe different
responses; their increase near onset does not make them the same quantity.
