\section{Introduction}
Transcranial electrical stimulation (tES) injects weak currents through scalp electrodes
\cite{ruffini2013}. Conventional protocols act directly at the frequencies of brain
function, from DC to a few hundred hertz
\cite{reato2010,liu2018,krause2023}. Its reach to deep targets is limited
by physics. Under the quasistatic approximation,
electric-field strength has no strict
three-dimensional interior maximum in homogeneous, isotropic, source-free tissue: its
maximum lies on a boundary, such as the scalp or a conductivity interface
\cite{heller1992}. Field strength is \textit{subharmonic}. Its value at a point
is at most its average over a ball centered there and contained in the uniform
region. A montage therefore cannot produce a strict local field maximum deep
inside that region. With spatially fixed linear
coupling to the membrane, the mean-square polarization is subharmonic too and obeys the
same restriction (\S\ref{sec:dc}). 

Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation aims at the focal modulation of deep brain
targets by controlling a nonlinear feature of the field, its \textit{envelope}
\cite{grossman2017}. The envelope of a signal $x$ is the modulus of its analytic
signal, $|x+i\hat x|$, where $\hat x$ is its Hilbert transform
\cite{gabor1946}; for two interfering kHz fields it is the slow amplitude modulation at
their difference frequency. The envelope excursion (its range over a beat period) and the
quadratic beat amplitude need not be
subharmonic and can have interior maxima (supplement, \S\ref{supp:interior-maxima}),
whereas the beat-period mean of the envelope remains subject to the restriction
(\S\ref{sec:dc}). %Spatial targeting therefore depends on which feature of the waveform
%the neurons detect.

To construct such a feature, two sinusoidal currents are injected at $f_1$ and
$f_2=f_1+\Delta f$ through separate electrode pairs, typically with $f_1\sim1$--$2$~kHz
and $\Delta f\sim1$--$100$~Hz, producing, where they overlap, an amplitude-modulated (AM)
electric field whose direction varies in time when the component fields are nonparallel. Changing the
currents steers the region of largest envelope modulation. Locally, the membrane polarization can be written as a carrier at the midpoint
frequency $f_c=(f_1+f_2)/2=f_1+\Delta f/2$, with a time-varying amplitude and phase,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
s(t)&=\varepsilon_1\cos(\omega_1 t)+\varepsilon_2\cos(\omega_2 t)
     =\mathcal A(t)\cos\!\big(\omega_c t+\varphi(t)\big),\\
\mathcal A(t)&=\sqrt{\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2
                    +2\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos(\Omega t)}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:am}
\end{equation}
 The transformation $s\rightarrow\mathcal A$ is nonlinear.  
Here $t$ is time and $\varepsilon_{1,2}$ are the tone amplitudes in mV after
field-to-membrane coupling (\S\ref{sec:coupling}). With $\omega_j=2\pi f_j$,
the carrier angular frequency is $\omega_c=2\pi f_c$ and
the beat angular frequency is $\Omega=\omega_2-\omega_1=2\pi\,\Delta f$.
The phase is
$\varphi(t)=\arg[\varepsilon_1e^{-i\Omega t/2}+\varepsilon_2e^{i\Omega t/2}]$
wherever $\mathcal A>0$; at envelope zeros it does not affect $s$.
%For equal tones, $\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2=\varepsilon$,
%$s(t)=2\varepsilon\cos(\Omega t/2)\cos(\omega_c t)$.
%Its nonnegative envelope, $\mathcal A(t)=2|\varepsilon\cos(\Omega t/2)|$,
%repeats at the beat frequency $\Delta f$.

The spectrum of $s$ contains only the two
applied lines. A linear, time-invariant system can attenuate or phase-shift them but
cannot create a new one. Passive membrane filtering therefore cannot demodulate TI
\cite{mirzakhalili2020}.
A nonlinear element is needed to mix the carriers into the
low-frequency spectrum, as in AM reception \cite{nahin2001}.


The mechanism requires three stages. \emph{Access} converts the extracellular electric field
into local membrane polarization. \emph{Detection}, or demodulation, mixes those
polarizations nonlinearly to generate the difference frequency. \emph{Amplification} is the
neuron's and recurrent circuit's response to that source. Strong coupling,
effective mixing, and resonant gain are distinct properties, even when the
same cellular structure contributes to several stages.

Somatic charging attenuates kHz fields, with the response also depending on cable
geometry and field coupling \cite{deitcher2017,eyal2016}. Esmaeilpour et al.\
\cite{esmaeilpour2021} already modeled TI sensitivity using a short, axon-like
membrane time, distinguishing sensitivity from selectivity mediated by
network adaptation. Cohen et al.'s conducting periaxonal space separates nodal and
internodal voltage dynamics and supports saltatory conduction
\cite{cohen2020periaxonal}; nodes and the sodium-channel-rich axon initial segment
(AIS) are candidate fast access sites \cite{kole2008ais}. We represent such a route by a
short effective membrane time, whose field-to-polarization transfer requires
calibration.

Detection requires that this polarization encounter a nonlinearity. Mirzakhalili
et al.\ \cite{mirzakhalili2020} demonstrated subthreshold nodal mixing in a modified
McIntyre--\allowbreak Richardson--\allowbreak Grill (MRG) myelinated-axon model
with Hodgkin--Huxley ion-channel dynamics. Cortical-neuron models also
show axonal excitation and conduction block under stronger fields \cite{wang2023}.
Luff et al.\ \cite{luff2024mixer} directly observed subthreshold difference-frequency
oscillations in individual neurons, including carriers near $5$~kHz. Mixing persisted
during synaptic blockade but was suppressed by blocking tetrodotoxin-sensitive sodium
channels. This establishes cellular detection without locating its compartment. Smooth
cellular responses admit a population-level second-order expansion.  A static
rate curve already supplies this through its curvature.

Amplification determines whether the generated beat appreciably changes activity.
Intrinsic currents, including the hyperpolarization-activated current $I_h$ and
M-type potassium current $I_M$, can tune a single neuron \cite{hutcheonyarom2000},
as in the cell- and state-dependent hippocampal TI responses modeled by de Palma
Aristides et al.\ in an unpublished study \cite{depalma2026ti}. Recurrent excitatory--inhibitory circuits supply another
resonance \cite{brunelhakim1999,brunelwang2003,ledoux2011}. In slices, pyramidal and
parvalbumin (PV) neurons respond differently, and most tested pyramidal cells
lose their preferential response to modulated over equal-peak unmodulated
carriers after synaptic blockade \cite{caldas2024}; simulations also find network
mediation \cite{karimi2024,esmaeilpour2021}. Reviews summarize these mechanisms
\cite{soroushi2025,wangw2026}. At human-comparable fields, primate recordings show
weaker effects than direct low-frequency stimulation, expressed mainly in spike
timing rather than mean rate \cite{vieira2024}.

Here we develop the receiver picture of Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}: cellular mixing followed
by recurrent gain, extending models of endogenous cross-frequency coupling
\cite{ruffini2025cfc,ruffini2025ham}. We work at the population level, with weak
stimulation-induced changes around ongoing activity that may already include spiking.
Direct carrier-evoked spiking and conduction block
\cite{opancar2025,peripheral2023,semenov2025} lie outside this expansion, and the
finite-amplitude network examples are identified as such.

Weak static detection produces two sources: a beat proportional to the product of the
carrier polarizations, and a steady, direct-current-like source proportional to their
summed squares. Cassar\`a et al.\ showed that this product law fixes the spatial
pattern of the beat at fixed field geometry, coupling, and neural state, so that
changing the current ratio only rescales it \cite{cassara2025part2}. What is new here concerns the fate of both sources in neural
tissue, followed across a Jansen--Rit (JR) neural mass column, the exact mean field of
quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) neurons, and its spiking network. 

Access, represented
by an effective membrane time, sets a kHz roll-off and input budgets conditional on the
assumed coupling. At the studied JR operating point, $0.1$--$0.3$~mV per carrier gives
TI source magnitudes about $2$--$5\%$ of those of transcranial alternating- or
direct-current stimulation (tACS, tDCS) at equal per-carrier and direct-input polarizations. The steady source is a stimulus in its own right, with sign set by detector state
and dynamics rather than carrier polarity. In QIF, weak fast carriers at fixed
synaptic input produce a steady rate shift governed by the rate-curve slope, even
where curvature predicts the opposite sign. At stronger input in the recurrent QIF
mean field, an unmodulated carrier matched in root-mean-square input current shows
that rectified drive alone can induce gamma without a beat. 

In the JR column, with its field-free operating point pinned, recurrent gain varies
independently of detector curvature. This network amplification grows near a Hopf
bifurcation and is shared with tACS. Under weak drive, the response
favors beat-locked rate modulation over mean-rate change. In one realistic head model, the current ratio leaves the beat pattern unchanged
but moves the envelope and mean-square centroids within the hippocampal target in
opposite directions, so predicted targeting depends on which feature the tissue detects.

 These results yield falsifiable
predictions: amplitude scaling, harmonic content, and equal-RMS controls test the
detector laws and separate steady from beat responses. The size of these effects in vivo depends on the
field-to-detector transfer at kHz frequencies, which remains to be calibrated. Within
that limit, the modeled cortical column behaves as a tuned receiver whose response is set in
turn by access, detection, and gain.



\section{Theory}

\subsection{A population rate curve supplies the nonlinearity}\label{sec:nmm}
A neural mass model describes a population through averaged variables
\cite{ruffini2025rosetta,jansen1995}. In the Jansen--Rit (JR) family, a linear synaptic
filter converts presynaptic firing rate $r$ into postsynaptic potential $u$,
\[
\ddot u+2a\dot u+a^2u=Aa\,r,
\]
with inverse synaptic time $a$ (s$^{-1}$) and PSP gain $A$ (mV); inhibitory synapses use
$b$ and $B$ in the same respective units. Summed postsynaptic potentials give the mean input $v$, which
a static transfer curve converts to firing rate \cite{wilsoncowan1972},
\begin{equation}
\Sig(v)=\frac{2e_0}{1+e^{\,\rho(v_0-v)}} .
\label{eq:sigmoid}
\end{equation}
Here $2e_0$ is the maximum rate, $v_0$ the inflection voltage, and $\rho$ the inverse
voltage scale (s$^{-1}$, mV, and mV$^{-1}$, respectively). Primes denote
derivatives with respect to voltage. Nonzero curvature makes this curve a mixer, while its slope sets
the local gain of the recurrent circuit.

The JR column couples pyramidal, excitatory-interneuron, and inhibitory-interneuron
populations, with field input to the pyramidal sigmoid and local field potential
(LFP) proxy $v=y_1-y_2$, where $y_1$ and $y_2$ are the excitatory and inhibitory
postsynaptic potentials onto the pyramidal population. Its external input rate is
$p$ (s$^{-1}$), and $C$ is the dimensionless connectivity scale.
Figure~\ref{fig:models} locates the field input, nonlinear detector, and
recurrent amplifier in the JR and QIF circuits.

\subsection{From applied field to a squared-envelope beat component}\label{sec:coupling}
We neglect propagation and induction in the extracellular field $E$ (V/m),
but retain finite membrane charging. The effective polarization $s$ (mV)
enters the population sigmoid after cellular access,
\begin{equation}
r=\Sig(v+s),\qquad
E(t)=E_1\cos\omega_1 t+E_2\cos\omega_2 t .
\label{eq:coupling}
\end{equation}
Soma, dendrites, AIS, nodes, and terminals contribute parallel inputs with
different couplings and response times. For nearby carriers centered at
$\omega_c=2\pi f_c$,
\begin{equation}
s\simeq\sum_k \boldsymbol\lambda^k_f(\omega_c)\!\cdot\!\mathbf E,\qquad
\boldsymbol\lambda^k_f(\omega)=\boldsymbol\lambda^k_0\,\lvert H_k(\omega)\rvert,
\label{eq:doors}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol\lambda^k_0$ is compartment $k$'s static polarization vector
(a length) and $H_k$ its access filter \cite{ruffini2025rosetta,galangadea2023}.
This amplitude-gain sum assumes aligned compartment phases; otherwise the
complex transfers must be summed. For one dominant entry point,
$\lambda_f=\lambda_0|H|$ and $\varepsilon_j=\lambda_f(\omega_j)E_j$.
Holding these post-access polarizations fixed isolates detection and gain;
at fixed field they inherit $H$'s frequency dependence.

If access is fast relative to both carriers, $|H(\omega_j)|\approx1$ and
$\lambda_f\approx\lambda_0$. At the AIS,
$\Sig(v+s)$ represents a spike-initiation threshold shifted from $v_0$ to
$v_0-s$, requiring no backward transmission to the soma. The conversion from
AIS polarization to $s$ and its response time require calibration. A static
detector additionally requires relaxation faster than the carrier.

Expanding about the field-free voltage $v^*$ (mV)
\cite{ruffini2025ham} gives
$\Sig(v^*+s)\approx\Sig(v^*)+\Sig'(v^*)s+\half\Sig''(v^*)s^2+\dots$.
Quadratic mixing leads when $|s|\ll1/\rho\approx1.8$~mV and
$\Sig''(v^*)\ne0$; larger excursions require the full curve.
The linear term preserves the carriers, while
$2\cos\omega_1t\cos\omega_2t=\cos\Omega t+\cos(\omega_1+\omega_2)t$
generates their difference. Averaging over the carrier removes the fast terms
before applying network gain; synaptic filtering suppresses those terms
downstream of detection. Since $\langle\cos^2\omega_jt\rangle_c=\half$,
\begin{equation}
\big\langle\Sig(v^*+s)\big\rangle_c
=\Sig(v^*)+\tfrac14\Sig''(v^*)\,(\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2)
+\tfrac12\Sig''(v^*)\,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos\Omega t+O(\varepsilon^4),
\label{eq:taylor}
\end{equation}
giving a steady source from the summed squares and a signed beat-source amplitude (s$^{-1}$)
\begin{equation}
Y_\Omega\simeq\half\,\Sig''(v^*)\,\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 .
\label{eq:demod}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon$ denotes a common small-amplitude scale and $\simeq$
denotes leading order in polarization throughout.

For a dynamic cellular response with synaptic input held fixed, define
the second-order coefficient $\chi^{(2)}$ through the complex source amplitude,
\begin{equation}
Y_\Omega\simeq\chi^{(2)}(\omega_2,-\omega_1)\,
\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_1,\qquad
r_\Omega(t)=\operatorname{Re}\!\left[Y_\Omega e^{i\Omega t}\right].
\label{eq:chi2}
\end{equation}
The generated rate $r_\Omega$ precedes recurrence, so $\chi^{(2)}$ has units
s$^{-1}$mV$^{-2}$. With this cosine-amplitude convention, its memoryless
limit is $\Sig''/2$. Cellular history, state, and response times can make
it complex and frequency-dependent; symmetry or cancellation can remove
second-order mixing, leaving higher orders.

The static detector predicts quadratic scaling, a vanishing source at the
inflection, and carrier independence at fixed polarization. At fixed field,
access attenuation raises the field required for a given source, consistent
with carrier-dependent thresholds \cite{opancar2025,peripheral2023}.

\subsection{Envelope oscillations and spatial weighting}\label{sec:envelope}
Envelope following and quadratic detection assign different spatial doses
to the same field. At position $\mathbf x$, let $\varepsilon_j(\mathbf x)$ be
the signed polarization of tone $j$ along a fixed direction. The nonnegative
envelope of the two-tone polarization is
\[
\mathcal A(\mathbf x,t)=\sqrt{\varepsilon_1(\mathbf x)^2+\varepsilon_2(\mathbf x)^2
+2\varepsilon_1(\mathbf x)\varepsilon_2(\mathbf x)\cos\Omega t}.
\]
At each location, low-pass filtering (LPF) removes the carrier terms and gives
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{LPF}[s^2]=\tfrac12\mathcal A^2
=\frac{\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2}{2}+\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2\cos\Omega t,
\label{eq:sqenv}
\end{equation}
with spatial arguments suppressed. The squared envelope is sinusoidal at
$\Omega$, but the envelope is not: for matched tones it is the rectified
cosine $\mathcal A(t)=2\varepsilon|\cos(\Omega t/2)|$.
The conventional TI map measures its excursion,
$\mathcal M[y]\equiv\max_t y-\min_t y$ \cite{grossman2017}.

Table~\ref{tab:transduction-maps} separates these geometric quantities from
detector gains; overbars denote beat-period means. An ideal envelope follower
with gain $c_1$ outputs $c_1\mathcal A$, whereas a smooth transfer $g$ with
nonzero curvature gives $g''(v^*)\mathcal A^2/4$ at weak input ($g=\Sig$ in JR).
The nonnegative mean-square map $\mathcal I=(\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2)/2$ therefore specifies neither excitation
nor suppression, and an envelope-excursion map alone specifies no mean output.
Envelope following is itself a response assumption.

on the detector. Averaging the slow signal over a beat period gives its mean.
Excursion means maximum minus minimum, not Fourier amplitude.}
\label{tab:transduction-maps}
\end{table}

The excursion maps in Table~\ref{tab:transduction-maps} are related pointwise by
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M}\big[\tfrac12\mathcal A^2\big]=\max(|\varepsilon_1|,|\varepsilon_2|)\;\mathcal{M}[\mathcal A].
\label{eq:reweight}
\end{equation}
The dominant polarization reweights the envelope map. Both maps can have a
central maximum, although the quadratic example in Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial_maps}a
is broader. A larger superficial dominant carrier degrades deep contrast:
for $(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2)=(10,1)$ superficially and $(0.5,0.5)$
at depth, the superficial/deep excursion ratio is $2$ for the envelope
and $40$ for the quadratic map. A sufficiently weak second carrier can
instead suppress the superficial product. Actual contrast requires the vector fields.

With fixed electrode pairs, frequencies, neural state, linear access, and
projection directions, write
$\varepsilon_j(\mathbf x)=I_j^{\rm pair}\mathcal P_j(\mathbf x)$,
where $I_j^{\rm pair}>0$ is the peak current of pair $j$ (mA) and $\mathcal P_j$
the signed polarization per current (mV/mA). Then
\[
\mathcal M[\tfrac12\mathcal A^2]
=2I_1^{\rm pair}I_2^{\rm pair}|\mathcal P_1\mathcal P_2|.
\]
Changing the current ratio therefore rescales the beat map without moving its maxima
or changing normalized maps and target/reference ratios
\cite{cassara2025part2}. Fixed detector coefficients and linear propagation
preserve the common current factor, even with dynamic mixing and heterogeneous
gains. Steering requires a changed carrier-field shape, for example through
current redistribution among electrodes at one frequency.

Current-ratio changes can still reshape the beat response through the steady source,
which weights $\mathcal P_1^2$ and $\mathcal P_2^2$ by the squared currents and can
thereby shift the operating points. Such state changes fall outside the fixed-state
argument above. Threshold effects also depart from the weak quadratic regime; the
$2\min$ steering law requires envelope following.

The product law permits an interior beat maximum. Its spatial weighting also
depends on local detector and network factors (Eq.~\eqref{eq:receiver}), so
resonance can compensate for an unfavorable product only where the source
is nonzero.

The scalar maps use a common fixed projection,
$\varepsilon_j=\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf E_j$ with tone fields $\mathbf E_j$; the beat changes phase
when either projection changes sign. The current-product scaling also holds
when a detector combines input from several compartments. Let $a_j^k$ be the
complex polarization of carrier $j$ in compartment $k$. A weak dynamic
detector has a bilinear beat source
$Y_\Omega=\sum_{kl}B_{kl}(\omega_2,-\omega_1)a_2^k\overline{a_1^l}$,
where $B_{kl}$ are fixed response coefficients and the overbar means complex
conjugation. A single scalar input is a rank-one specialization. Linear
access gives $a_j^k=I_j^{\rm pair}b_j^k$, with $b_j^k$ the polarization per
unit current, so the common factor $I_1^{\rm pair}I_2^{\rm pair}$ and its
fixed spatial pattern survive without a universal projection vector.

\subsection{Detector laws and harmonic signatures}\label{sec:transduction}
For a narrowband input $s(t)=\mathcal A(t)\cos\omega_c t$, a memoryless
nonlinearity $g$ has low-frequency output given by its carrier-cycle average,
\[
Y_{\rm LF}(\mathcal A)=(2\pi)^{-1}\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!g(v^*+\mathcal A\cos\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta,
\]
where $\theta$ is carrier phase. For smooth $g$, averaging removes the odd powers
in the weak-input expansion,
\begin{equation}
Y_{\rm LF}(\mathcal A)=g(v^*)+\tfrac14 g''(v^*)\mathcal A^2+\tfrac1{64}g^{(4)}(v^*)\mathcal A^4+\cdots
\label{eq:cycavg}
\end{equation}
Thus smooth detection with nonzero curvature is quadratic at weak input.
A threshold detector instead gives
$\pi^{-1}\arccos(\delta/\mathcal A)$ for envelope amplitude
$\mathcal A>\delta$ and zero below, where $\delta>0$ is its offset.
Other rectifier laws are compared in the supplement (\S\ref{supp:harmonics}).

At matched tones, envelope following is linear in amplitude with
second/fundamental harmonic ratio $1/5$. Quadratic detection gives only
the steady term and fundamental; its quartic correction gives
$|Y_{2\Omega}|/|Y_\Omega|\approx|c_4/c_2|\varepsilon^2$ when the nonzero
quadratic term dominates, with $c_2=g''/4$ and $c_4=g^{(4)}/64$.
A threshold detector is silent for
$2\varepsilon\le\delta$ and has an amplitude-dependent ratio above onset.
The full harmonic series and mixed-detector inversion are supplementary
(\S\ref{supp:harmonics}).

\input{planning/parts/harmonic_details}
\fi

In the linear network regime, gain $G$ modifies the detector ratio
$\mathcal H_{\rm det}$, giving the measured ratio
\[
\mathcal H_{\rm meas}=\mathcal H_{\rm det}|G(2\Omega)/G(\Omega)|.
\]
Near resonance it can suppress an envelope follower's second harmonic,
making it appear quadratic. Amplitude sweeps and gain correction are
therefore needed; a threshold detector can match one harmonic ratio, so that
ratio alone cannot identify the detector.

\subsection{The steady source has a state-dependent sign}\label{sec:dc}
Detection converts nonnegative mean-square polarization into a signed steady
source; recurrence converts that source into a mean response. ``DC-like''
describes this source, not the applied field, which has no DC component.
At matched polarizations $\varepsilon>0$,
the envelope and quadratic means are $4\varepsilon/\pi$ and
$\varepsilon^2$ before detector gains (supplement, \S\ref{supp:envelope-proof}). Either ideal detector gives a mean
output with one carrier, but no beat; adaptation can alter the mean response.

The static JR source is
\[
Y_{\rm DC}(\mathbf x,\mathbf n)
=\frac{\Sig''(v^*_{\mathbf x})}{2}\,\mathcal I(\mathbf x,\mathbf n),
\qquad
\mathcal I(\mathbf x,\mathbf n)
=\frac{\varepsilon_1(\mathbf x,\mathbf n)^2+
\varepsilon_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf n)^2}{2}\ge0,
\]
where $v^*_{\mathbf x}$ is the local baseline and $\mathbf n$ the common
unit cell orientation. Convex and concave rate curves give positive and
negative sources, respectively; the quadratic source vanishes at the inflection
and in the saturated tails.
Reversing either carrier or $\mathbf n$ leaves the squares unchanged;
other orientations change magnitudes but not the sign at fixed curvature.
Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_maps}c shows both signs on one positive normalization.
At the pinned JR state, $v^*=8.30$~mV, the source lowers the local rate
because $\Sig''(v^*)<0$.
This curvature rule applies to static detection; dynamic rectification and
recurrence can change the sign of the mean response.
The same operating-point dependence enters the multitone sigmoid expansion for
endogenous infraslow ($<0.1$~Hz) mixing in coupled Jansen--Rit models \cite{Just2026mixing}.

\label{sec:opp}
The Population Excitation--Inhibition Index (PEIX) \cite{ruffini2025cfc}
measures signed departure from maximal sigmoid slope, positive above the
inflection and negative below; its definition and curvature identities are
in Appendix~\ref{app:peix}. Changes in the operating point $v^*$ can
therefore scale or reverse the static JR source.
Dynamic detectors have their own state-dependent coefficients and susceptibilities, without
a universal PEIX sign rule; state sweeps isolate detection only if recurrent
gain is also measured.

Carrier averaging retains the beat cross term; a beat-period average removes
it, leaving intensities without cross-frequency cancellation. Where
$Y_{\rm DC}\ne0$,
$|Y_\Omega/Y_{\rm DC}|=2|\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2|/
(\varepsilon_1^2+\varepsilon_2^2)\le1$, with equality at matched magnitudes.
A common access factor cancels. In the superficial/deep example of \S\ref{sec:envelope}, $\mathcal I$ is
$50.5$ superficially and $0.25$ at depth, a $202$-fold contrast;
the corresponding beat/DC ratios are $0.20$ and $1$.

In a homogeneous, isotropic, source-free conductor, the potentials $\phi_j$,
field components $\mathbf E_j=-\nabla\phi_j$, and polarizations
$\varepsilon_j=\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf E_j$ for spatially fixed
coupling $\boldsymbol\lambda$ are harmonic. Consequently the mean-square polarization
$\mathcal I$ obeys $\nabla^2\mathcal I=\sum_j|\nabla\varepsilon_j|^2\ge0$, so it is subharmonic, with no strict interior maximum. Its maximum
lies on the region's boundary, including conductivity interfaces, regardless
of montage. With spatially constant nonzero detection, the same holds for
$|Y_{\rm DC}|$; negative curvature makes the boundary source most negative.
Spatially varying access, orientation, or detector coefficients require a
weighted map, and the restriction does not extend to the physiological mean
response or an indefinite multicompartment DC form.

The envelope mean is also subharmonic: at each beat phase it is a norm of
harmonic components, and phase averaging preserves that property (supplement,
\S\ref{supp:envelope-proof}).
\ifjne\else
\input{planning/parts/envelope_proof}
\fi
By contrast,
$\nabla^2(\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2)=2\nabla\varepsilon_1\cdot\nabla\varepsilon_2$
has unrestricted sign. Within this homogeneous scalar model, interior
selectivity must come from the oscillatory response, not either mean map.

With isotropic orientations and orientation-independent access and curvature,
orthogonal fields cancel the averaged beat while retaining steady intensity
(supplement, \S\ref{supp:orientation}).

\input{planning/parts/orientation_average}
\fi

An unmodulated carrier at the same local RMS polarization gives the same
second-order mean shift if its DC mixing coefficient equals those of both
TI carriers. This motivates the equal-RMS carrier control in
\S\ref{sec:qif-methods}. For a dynamic detector, the coefficient must vary
negligibly over the carrier interval.

\subsection{Comparison with direct tDCS and tACS}\label{sec:direct-comparison}
We compare TI's steady and beat sources with transcranial direct-current
stimulation (tDCS) and tACS at $\Omega$, respectively, using polarizations
at the same sigmoid entry point. For equal TI tones
$\varepsilon_1=\varepsilon_2=\varepsilon>0$,
\[
Y_{\rm DC}^{\rm TI}\simeq Y_\Omega^{\rm TI}
\simeq \frac12\Sig''(v^*)\varepsilon^2.
\]
A constant polarization $\varepsilon_{\rm D}$ or sinusoid
$\varepsilon_{\rm A}\cos\Omega t$ instead gives
\[
Y_{\rm DC}^{\rm tDCS}\simeq\Sig'(v^*)\varepsilon_{\rm D},
\qquad
Y_\Omega^{\rm tACS}\simeq\Sig'(v^*)\varepsilon_{\rm A}.
\]
Here $\varepsilon_{\rm D}$ is signed and negative $\varepsilon_{\rm A}$
denotes phase reversal. The equivalent direct polarization matching either
TI source is
\[
\varepsilon_{\rm eq}
=\frac{\Sig''(v^*)}{2\Sig'(v^*)}\,\varepsilon^2,
\qquad
\frac{\Sig''(v^*)}{\Sig'(v^*)}
=-\rho\tanh\!\left[\frac{\rho(v^*-v_0)}2\right].
\]
Sigmoid steepness and operating voltage determine the ratio; the rate ceiling
cancels, and the quadratic TI sources vanish at the inflection.

For the JR parameters $\rho=0.56$~mV$^{-1}$, $v_0=6$~mV,
$e_0=2.5$~s$^{-1}$, and the pinned operating point $v^*=8.30$~mV,
$\Sig'=0.4745$~s$^{-1}$mV$^{-1}$ and
$\Sig''=-0.1508$~s$^{-1}$mV$^{-2}$. With each direct-input amplitude
equal to the per-carrier TI amplitude, the source-magnitude ratio is
$|\varepsilon_{\rm eq}|/\varepsilon
=0.1589\,\varepsilon/(1~\mathrm{mV})$:
$1.59\%$ at $0.1$~mV and $4.77\%$ at $0.3$~mV, rounded to $2$--$5\%$.
At $0.3$~mV per carrier, the equivalent direct polarization is
$-0.0143$~mV, or $-14.3~\mu$V. The negative sign specifies a suppressive
local source for tDCS and an inverted source phase for tACS.

Matching the total TI peak instead uses direct amplitude $2\varepsilon$
and halves these ratios. RMS matching uses tACS amplitude
$\sqrt2\varepsilon$ or tDCS magnitude $\varepsilon$, because TI's RMS
polarization is $\varepsilon$. The tACS quadratic steady source,
$\Sig''\varepsilon_{\rm A}^2/4$, then equals TI's for this static detector.
Equal electrode currents need not match polarization: access differs among
DC, beat and carrier frequencies, and among entry compartments.

At a common state and entry point, matched steady sources share the
zero-frequency transfer, and matched beat and tACS sources share the transfer
at $\Omega$. These transfers differ, and rectification of the amplified TI
beat can add a mean shift.
Source ratios therefore imply neither universal physiological-effect ratios
nor equal spatial responses.

\subsection{Amplification: the synaptic low-pass and the network resonance}\label{sec:carrier}
Synaptic filtering suppresses the carrier after the nonlinearity has generated
the beat. Each JR excitatory synapse has transfer function
$H_s(\omega)=Aa/(i\omega+a)^2$ with PSP gain $A$, a second-order low-pass with characteristic
frequency $a/(2\pi)\approx16$~Hz. It passes a $10$~Hz component with relative
amplitude about $0.7$ but reduces a $100$~Hz component to about $2.5\%$. Unless the
carrier itself excites a responsive mode, the loop therefore reads predominantly the
generated low-frequency drive.

% fig:carrier moved to Results (Theory is equations-only)

The recurrent circuit selects the recovered beat. Linearizing about the JR
fixed point gives a leading pair $\mu_\pm=-\gamma\pm i\omega_0$, with
modal damping $\gamma$ and frequency $\omega_0$. On the stable-focus side of a
simple Hopf bifurcation, $\gamma\to0^+$ and the quality factor
$\mathcal Q=\omega_0/(2\gamma)$ grows. A constant-numerator two-pole
approximation to the complex susceptibility $G$ gives
\begin{equation}
|G(\Omega)|\propto\big[(\omega_0^2+\gamma^2-\Omega^2)^2+(2\gamma\Omega)^2\big]^{-1/2},
\qquad |G(\omega_0)|\propto\frac{1}{2\gamma\omega_0}
\quad(\gamma\ll\omega_0),
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
an approximately Lorentzian power peak near $\omega_0$ with $1/\gamma$
growth at a simple Hopf with nonzero input--output projections.\footnote{
The eigenvalue frequency $\omega_0$ differs from the canonical oscillator's
undamped frequency $\sqrt{\omega_0^2+\gamma^2}$ and peak
$\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}$ ($\omega_0>\gamma$).
Frequency-dependent projections can further shift the peak.}
This weak-drive law requires stability after the rectified mean shift.
On the oscillatory side, entrainment instead means phase capture of an autonomous rhythm.

Write $D$ for the detector's beat-source coefficient: for the JR rate source,
$D=\half\Sig''(v^*)$, half the local sigmoid curvature. Combining access,
detection, and amplification gives
\begin{equation}
R_\Omega\simeq G(\Omega;\gamma)\;D\;\lambda_f(\omega_1)E_1\,\lambda_f(\omega_2)E_2 ,
\label{eq:receiver}
\end{equation}
% Retain the former spatial-equation label as an alias of the general receiver.
\makeatletter\begingroup\edef\@currentlabel{\theequation}\label{eq:spatial}\endgroup\makeatother
where $\lambda_f$ maps field to local carrier polarization, $D$ converts the
product of those polarizations into a beat drive, and $G$ maps that drive to the
complex observed response, whose measured amplitude is $A_\Omega=|R_\Omega|$.
For JR, $G$ is the response to a generated rate drive.
A static detector has carrier-independent $D$ at fixed polarization;
a dynamic detector can retain frequency dependence. A carrier sweep and a beat sweep
therefore constrain different stages rather than a single effective gain.

Resonance requires a beat source. Under additive forcing about its zero-amplitude
equilibrium, the cubic Hopf normal form has no quadratic mixing term.
A large resonant susceptibility alone therefore does not establish beat generation;
nonlinear input maps or readouts must be specified separately.
In the calibrated noise-free Stuart--Landau networks tested in \cite{Ruffini2026iso},
first-order rate transmission and readout yielded negligible infraslow BOLD power.

\subsection{Carrier access depends on the effective membrane response}\label{sec:khz}
Carrier independence at fixed polarization does not imply independence at fixed
applied field. As an isopotential access model, consider
$H_m(\omega)=1/(1+i\omega\tau_m)$, with effective time constant $\tau_m$.
This is the response of a point membrane to injected current; a field coupled
through a cable need not have the same transfer.
For nearby carriers, the two polarizations acquire nearly the same attenuation.
The quadratic beat then satisfies
\begin{equation}
\frac{|Y_\Omega(f_c)|}{|Y_\Omega^{\rm direct}|}
\simeq |H_m(\omega_c)|^2
=\frac{1}{1+(\omega_c\tau_m)^2}
\sim\frac{1}{(\omega_c\tau_m)^2}
\quad(\omega_c\tau_m\gg1),
\label{eq:khz}
\end{equation}
where $Y_\Omega^{\rm direct}$ is the beat generated with the same static coupling
and no access attenuation. This filter belongs either in the field-to-polarization
map or in the membrane equation that computes that polarization.

For an illustrative somatic $\tau_m=16$~ms, $|H_m|^2$ is about
$10^{-4}$, $2.5\times10^{-5}$, and $10^{-6}$ at $1$, $2$, and $10$~kHz.
The assumed $0.2$~ms case gives $0.39$ and $0.14$ at $1$ and $2$~kHz
(Fig.~\ref{fig:khz}); this transfer has not been measured under TI at an
AIS or node. Table~\ref{tab:doors} distinguishes measured responses,
assumed access, and downstream filtering. Axonal decay alone specifies
neither field coupling nor a TI cutoff.

A sufficiently long passive cable with a sealed end in a uniform axial field
has quadratic-beat factors $|Y_\Omega(f_c)|/|Y_\Omega^{\rm direct}|$ near $10^{-2}$
at $1$~kHz and $10^{-3}$ at $10$~kHz for $\tau_m=16$~ms, exceeding the point-membrane
values.

QIF computes access in its membrane equation, so current input needs no additional
filter. Shorter access improves transmission but determines neither mixing nor
recurrent gain.

\subsection{The exact mean field extends the static detector}\label{sec:dynamic}
For all-to-all quadratic integrate-and-fire (QIF) neurons with Lorentzian
excitabilities, the Montbri\'o--Paz\'o--Roxin (MPR) equations govern physical
firing rate $r$ and dimensionless mean voltage $v$ \cite{montbrio2015}.
Adding dynamic synapses gives a second-generation neural mass (NMM2)
\cite{ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}. At fixed synaptic input, weak-carrier
steady rectification follows the static rate curve's curvature at low frequencies
and its slope at high frequencies. The population equations are
\begin{equation}
\tau_m\dot r=\frac{\Delta}{\pi\tau_m}+2rv,\qquad
\tau_m\dot v=v^2+\bar\eta-(\pi\tau_m r)^2+I.
\label{eq:mpr}
\end{equation}
Here $\tau_m$ is membrane time, dots denote physical-time derivatives,
$\bar\eta$ and $\Delta>0$ are the excitability center and half-width, and
$I$ is dimensionless input, including recurrent drive when present.
At constant total drive $Q=\bar\eta+I$, the equilibrium solution \cite{ruffini2021nmm2}
gives $v=-\Delta/(2\pi\tau_m r)$ and
\begin{equation}
\tau_m r=F(Q)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{Q+\sqrt{Q^2+\Delta^2}}{2}}.
\label{eq:Fmpr}
\end{equation}
Primes on $F$ denote drive derivatives. Below the inflection at
$Q=\Delta/\sqrt3$, curvature reflects recruitment across threshold;
above it, the concave square-root rate of active cells. This nonlinearity
exists without recurrence and grows without bound, unlike the JR sigmoid.

At fixed input, $r$ and $v$ relax on a population timescale $\tau_{\rm rel}$.
When input varies on the synaptic timescale $\tau_s\gg\tau_{\rm rel}$,
we replace their differential equations by $r\simeq F(Q)/\tau_m$
\cite{ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}. The synapses keep evolving and
drive the rate (Eq.~\eqref{eq:slow_synapse}, Appendix~\ref{app:models}).
Carrier tracking additionally requires $\omega_c\tau_{\rm rel}\ll1$. A population can follow synaptic input quasistatically
yet respond dynamically to kHz stimulation.
Recurrent excitatory--inhibitory circuits resonate without intrinsic
cellular resonance \cite{brunelhakim1999,brunelwang2003}. Such synaptic
modes survive the fast-membrane limit, whereas modes requiring finite
membrane dynamics need not.

\paragraph{Field coupling and steady recurrent gain.}
For one self-coupled population, $I=I_b+\tau_mJu+\lambda_I E$, where $I_b$ is
the normalized background, $J$ is the dimensionless self-coupling, and $u$
is the gain-normalized synaptic potential (Eq.~\eqref{eq:slow_synapse}).
For fixed field direction, $\lambda_I=\kappa_E/I_s$, where $\kappa_E$ is equivalent
current per field and $I_s$ the current unit; a cellular voltage-polarization
coefficient requires impedance conversion.
At equilibrium the normalized potential satisfies $u_*=r_*$. To first order, a field increment changes both direct and recurrent input,
\begin{equation}
\delta r=\frac{F'(Q_*)}{\tau_m}\big(\lambda_I\delta E+\tau_mJ\delta r\big),
\end{equation}
so
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial r_*}{\partial E}
=\frac{\lambda_I F'(Q_*)}{\tau_m[1-JF'(Q_*)]}.
\label{eq:slow-field-gain}
\end{equation}
This stationary derivative requires no timescale separation.
At the same equilibrium drive $Q_*$, clamping $u$ gives $\lambda_I F'(Q_*)/\tau_m$.
At stable equilibria with $JF'(Q_*)<1$, positive feedback amplifies this response by
$1/[1-JF'(Q_*)]$; negative feedback suppresses it.
The bare curve $F$ takes total drive as its argument. A rate curve fitted against external drive after
recurrence settles includes that feedback; adding the same loop counts it twice.
JR-like descriptions retain the gain when input conversion, local slope, and feedback
match \cite{ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}; between-population connections do not
automatically specify that within-population self-coupling.

Outside the adiabatic limit, voltage squaring mixes the carriers into a slow drive before the rate responds. For an uncoupled population under a weak current
$I_{\rm ac}\cos\omega t$, write the mean-rate shift as
$I_{\rm ac}^2\kappa_{\rm DC}/\tau_m$. The low-frequency limit is $\kappa_{\rm DC}\to F''(Q)/4$. At high frequencies,
$\kappa_{\rm DC}\sim F'(Q)/[2(\omega\tau_m)^2]$.
The slow response follows curvature and can be negative above the inflection.
The fast DC coefficient instead follows the positive slope $F'$, largest at the
inflection, without a PEIX-based sign reversal: voltage excursions of order
$I_{\rm ac}/(\omega\tau_m)$ generate a positive input shift through $v^2$.
A shorter membrane time can therefore change both the magnitude and sign
of the mean response at a given physical carrier frequency.

Normalizing by squared voltage excursion removes membrane access and gives
the fast-carrier limit $F'/2$. For two nearby current tones, voltage squaring
gives a beat drive proportional to $I_1I_2/(\omega_1\omega_2\tau_m^2)$,
where $I_j$ are the tone amplitudes.
The dynamic rate equation further attenuates the carriers after mixing;
that additional carrier-frequency attenuation is not applied to the generated
beat, which is filtered at its own frequency.
The crossover is derived in
Appendix~\ref{app:mpr-coefficients}; fixed-voltage coefficients and carrier
exponents are supplementary (\S\ref{supp:dynamic-details}).

In membrane-time units the coefficient of $v^2$ is one; in this cellular realization
of quadratic detection it plays the role of $\Sig''/2$, and $G$ includes the voltage
and rate dynamics that convert the drive into a rate beat. Other smooth cellular
mechanisms share the weak-input product law without sharing its coefficient, phase,
or carrier dependence.

\section{Methods}

\paragraph{Response terminology.}\label{sec:vocab-methods}
A \emph{forced response} is the beat-driven component measured by lock-in
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}); a purely forced regime requires stability under
stimulation. \emph{Entrainment} means frequency and phase capture of a self-sustained
oscillator \cite{pikovsky2001}, which our frequency-dominance screen does not
establish. \emph{Gating} means modulation of a persisting rhythm's amplitude
\cite{canolty2010,tort2010}, which a rate fold alone does not establish. Detailed criteria
are in the supplement (\S\ref{sec:vocab}).

\subsection{Stimulation waveforms and matched-source controls}\label{sec:waveforms}
Dense perturbative sweeps use the three-line waveform
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\varepsilon\,[\,1+m\cos\Omega t\,]\cos\omega_c t,\qquad \omega_c=2\pi f_c ,
\label{eq:amsur}
\end{equation}
with dimensionless modulation depth $m$. This AM surrogate parameterizes dense resonance
and coupling sweeps by one amplitude. It contains a central carrier line absent from
genuine TI but matches its leading beat source, $Y_\Omega=\half\Sig''\varepsilon^2m$.
For matched genuine tones $\mathrm{LPF}[s^2]=\varepsilon^2(1+\cos\Omega t)$, whereas for
the $m=1$ surrogate
$\mathrm{LPF}[s^2]=\varepsilon^2(\tfrac34+\cos\Omega t+\tfrac14\cos2\Omega t)$, so the
surrogate changes the mean source and adds a second harmonic at $25\%$ of the
fundamental.

Genuine tones are used for the demodulation, curvature, square-law, laminar, and kHz
demonstrations, and wherever mean shifts or harmonics enter the comparison, including
timing versus rate and the TI/tACS and NMM2 endpoint controls. Open-loop agreement at
the beat is checked at one operating point and does not imply equal driven operating
states.

\subsection{Amplitude scaling and harmonic measurements}\label{sec:transduction-methods}
At fixed coupling and operating state, we measure the fundamental and second
harmonic across an amplitude sweep to distinguish linear from quadratic scaling.

A software lock-in projects the readout $v$ onto sine and cosine at $\Omega$; its
finite window suppresses, but does not eliminate, neighboring frequencies. For Hann
weights $w_k$, sample times $t_k$, voltages $v_k=v(t_k)$, and weighted mean
$\bar v=\sum_k w_kv_k/\sum_k w_k$, the quadratures are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
L_{\cos}=\frac{2}{\sum_k w_k}\sum_k w_k\,(v_k-\bar v)\cos\Omega t_k,\quad
L_{\sin}=\frac{2}{\sum_k w_k}\sum_k w_k\,(v_k-\bar v)\sin\Omega t_k,\\
A_\Omega=\sqrt{L_{\cos}^2+L_{\sin}^2},
\end{gathered}
\label{eq:lockin}
\end{equation}
where $k$ indexes samples. Over long windows, a test tone
$v=A_{\rm test}\cos(\Omega t+\theta_0)$ with amplitude $A_{\rm test}$
and phase $\theta_0$ gives
$(L_{\cos},L_{\sin})\to A_{\rm test}(\cos\theta_0,-\sin\theta_0)$,
so $A_\Omega$ returns physical amplitude, twice the Fourier-coefficient magnitude.
Mean subtraction suppresses leakage from the larger baseline (supplement,
\S\ref{supp:lockin}).

\subsection{Exact next-generation mean field (NMM2 PING)}\label{sec:nmm2-methods}
In the pyramidal--interneuron network gamma (PING) circuit, second-order synapses
couple excitatory and inhibitory MPR populations
\cite{montbrio2015,ruffini2021nmm2,clusella2023}, in the Rosetta Stone
formulation shared with JR and laminar models \cite{ruffini2025rosetta}.
For population $X\in\{E,I\}$, $r_X$ is physical rate (ms$^{-1}$),
$v_X$ dimensionless mean voltage, and $\tau_X$ membrane time (ms).
With dots denoting physical-time derivatives,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\tau_E\dot r_E&=\Delta/(\pi\tau_E)+2r_Ev_E, &\quad
\tau_E\dot v_E&=v_E^2+\bar\eta-(\pi\tau_E r_E)^2+\tau_E J\,(u_E-u_I)+I_E,\\[2pt]
\tau_I\dot r_I&=\Delta/(\pi\tau_I)+2r_Iv_I, &\quad
\tau_I\dot v_I&=v_I^2+\bar\eta-(\pi\tau_I r_I)^2+\tau_I J\,(u_E-2u_I),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:nmm2-ping}
\end{equation}
where $u_E=\hat K_a[r_E]$ and $u_I=\hat K_g[r_I]$ are the gain-normalized
postsynaptic potentials (ms$^{-1}$), as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:slow_synapse}. The unit-gain filters have time constants
$\tau_a$ and $\tau_g$ for the excitatory and inhibitory sources, respectively.
Equation~\eqref{eq:nmm2-ping} includes
the coupling in $J$ and the pathway weights. The weight magnitudes
$(w_{EE},w_{EI},w_{IE},w_{II})=(1,1,1,2)$ are indexed by target then source.
In the schematic, $\mathcal U_j$ denotes
neuron $j$'s total synaptic and field input, and $J^*$ the coupling-induced Hopf.

The background $\bar\eta$ controls a supercritical Hopf that opens a gamma cycle,
analogous to the JR alpha Hopf. The field enters the excitatory voltage equation as
$I_E=\lambda_I E$ \cite{ruffini2021nmm2}, with instantaneous coupling $\lambda_I$ in
normalized drive per V/m. The two tones, at $f_c\mp\Delta f/2$, have amplitude
$A_f$ in the dimensionless drive units of $\bar\eta$; $f_c=300$~Hz, and $\Delta f$
is swept through gamma. The membrane integrates each tone into an excursion
$A_f/(\omega\tau_E)$, which $v_E^2$ rectifies, so access uses $\tau_m=\tau_E$ without
an assumed sigmoid.

Integration uses fixed-step RK4 ($\Delta t=0.05$~ms), reading out the lock-in
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}) of $r_E$ at $\Delta f$ over the $(\Delta f, \bar\eta)$ plane.
Parameters (Rosetta Stone PING \cite{ruffini2025rosetta}) are $\Delta=1$, global
coupling $J=15$, $\tau_E=15$, $\tau_I=7.5$, $\tau_a=10$, $\tau_g=2.5$~ms;
$\bar\eta$ is swept through the supercritical Hopf at $\bar\eta_H=1.110$
($f_0=54.0$~Hz), located by bisection on the leading eigenvalue of the mean-field
Jacobian. The simulations retain these finite membrane times, whereas
Eq.~\eqref{eq:slow_synapse} characterizes the fast-membrane limit.

The resonance map varies $\bar\eta$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:nmm2_map}). The coupling sweep varies
$J$ toward $J^*$ at fixed $\bar\eta$, locating $J^*$ by the same eigenvalue bisection,
and measures the peak rate lock-in over $\Delta f$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:nmm2_jcurve}). Unlike
in JR, this sweep does not pin the operating point. At $\bar\eta=0$, continuation tracks
the critical pair defining $\gamma$, even when another mode dominates. The linear
reference includes the state-dependent source and full transfer; grids, amplitude
checks, and normalizations are in the supplement (\S\ref{supp:ping-runs}).

\subsection{QIF spiking network (microscale of NMM2)}\label{sec:qif-methods}
We simulate a finite-cutoff approximation to the all-to-all QIF network underlying
Eq.~\eqref{eq:nmm2-ping} \cite{montbrio2015,ruffini2025rosetta,clusella2023},
with $N=8000$ neurons per population. Excitabilities are
deterministic quantiles of Lorentzian$(\bar\eta,\Delta)$. Each single-neuron voltage $V_j$ spikes at $V_{\rm peak}=100$
and resets to $-V_{\rm peak}$ (model units). Empirical rates drive the synapses. Genuine
two-tone input enters excitatory membranes because spiking and threshold dynamics need
not preserve the surrogate's equivalence beyond perturbation theory.

At $f_c=300$~Hz we test $A_f=10$ and $35$ per tone below and above the
field-free Hopf, at $\bar\eta=0.85$ ($55$~Hz beat) and $1.5$ ($42$~Hz).
The equal-RMS mean-field control uses one $300$~Hz carrier of peak amplitude
$\sqrt2 A_f$. Two-tone integrations check the stability prediction from the
carrier-averaged steady source (\S\ref{app:qif-estimate}).

Euler integration uses $0.0025$~ms steps. We report rate modulation depth
$A_\Omega/\langle r_E\rangle$ because an off/on ratio would divide by the field-free
floor. Single-neuron locking at $\Delta f$ is measured by pairwise phase consistency
(PPC), unbiased in spike count \cite{vinck2010}, for neurons with at least ten
spikes per window.
Spectra and rate folds separate components without establishing phase capture.
Step, population, window, and spike-cutoff checks test the finite-cutoff network
(\S\ref{supp:qif-protocol}). A cutoff-free theta-neuron version tests step,
population, and window sensitivity against a predeclared $5\%$ rate-depth
tolerance (\S\ref{supp:theta-pilot}).
Quantitative convergence remains unestablished, so the finite-network
comparison is qualitative.

increases modulation of $r_P=\Sig(v+s)$ by $13.2$-fold ($26$ to $339$~mHz),
following $1/\gamma$ before saturation. The same rate's mean shift grows
$4.4$-fold ($-1.8$ to $-7.7$~mHz), remaining below $0.2\%$ of its
$3.9$~Hz baseline (Fig.~\ref{fig:timing}). Their magnitude ratio rises
from $14.5$ to $58$ near $\gamma=1$~s$^{-1}$, then falls to $44$ as
the amplified oscillation rectifies. In this weakly driven regime, a small
mean change accompanies larger beat modulation, consistent with primate observations
\cite{vieira2024}, with both compared in rate units. The surrogate
comparison is supplementary (\S\ref{supp:waveform-checks}).

The detector's steady and beat sources meet different transfers:
$|G(\omega_0)|\propto1/(2\gamma\omega_0)$ grows toward the Hopf, while
$|G(0)|\propto1/(\omega_0^2+\gamma^2)$ stays finite. The amplified beat
also rectifies, adding a mean shift quadratic in its amplitude that can
grow as $1/\gamma^2$ before saturation. A bounded zero-frequency transfer
therefore does not bound the full mean shift.

\paragraph{Comparison with tACS.}\label{sec:tacs-results}
TI and tACS share the linear amplifier, so matching their generated in-band
drives at a common state matches their linear outputs; resonance alone gives
TI no efficiency advantage.
For the AM surrogate with amplitude $\varepsilon_{\rm TI}$ and modulation depth
$m$, and a tACS input of amplitude $\varepsilon_{\rm tACS}$, the ratio of voltage
lock-in amplitudes in linear response is
\[
\mathcal R_{\rm lin}
=\frac{|\Sig''(v^*)|\,\varepsilon_{\rm TI}^2m}
{2\Sig'(v^*)\,\varepsilon_{\rm tACS}}.
\]
The common linear transfer cancels. At $v^*=8.30$~mV,
$\varepsilon_{\rm TI}=0.3$~mV, $m=1$, and
$\varepsilon_{\rm tACS}=0.1$~mV give a predicted ratio of $0.143$.
Genuine two-tone responses depart from this prediction near the Hopf. When the leading sources are matched instead, the TI/tACS output ratios are $0.90$--$0.99$ at the three tested couplings. The larger direct input saturates earlier and shifts the operating state, which field-free pinning does not cancel. Numerical controls for these unequal-input comparisons are in the supplement (\S\ref{supp:tacs-saturation}). Equal-per-carrier, total-peak, and RMS matching are distinguished in \S\ref{sec:direct-comparison}.

\subsection{PING amplifies the dynamic QIF beat source}\label{sec:nmm2-results}
With genuine $300$~Hz tones entering E current, the PING rate $r_E$
shows forced gamma resonance below the Hopf and a beat response superposed
on autonomous gamma above it (Fig.~\ref{fig:nmm2_res}).
The resonance grows through the Hopf and shifts with state, forming a diagonal
ridge in the $(\Delta f,\bar\eta)$ plane of Fig.~\ref{fig:nmm2_map}, unlike
the JR example \cite{clusella2023}.

At $300$~Hz, voltage excursions mix through $v_E^2$ on the fast side of
the detector's crossover (Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover}a). Each current tone of amplitude $I_j$
produces a voltage phasor $I_j/(i\omega_j\tau_E)$, giving a beat source
$I_1I_2/(\tau_E^2\omega_1\omega_2)$ in the equation for $\tau_E\dot v_E$.
After division by $\tau_E$ and propagation through the network transfer at
$\Omega$, it matches the full second-order response at four stable operating
points within $1.1\%$ at $300$~Hz, $0.1\%$ at $1$~kHz, and $0.03\%$
at $2$~kHz (Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover}b).

The unpinned PING coupling sweep also approaches a gamma Hopf, but two
modes compete for the response peak. Near the Hopf, the critical mode's
linear gain has a fitted damping exponent of $1.02$, consistent with the
$1/\gamma$ law; the full-sweep exponent is $0.71$. At finite amplitude the response departs from the linear
reference and saturates, while the driven endpoint remains attracting.
The mode tracking, amplitude checks, and their numerical ranges accompany
the coupling curve in the supplement (\S\ref{supp:ping-coupling-results}).
Thus JR and PING share the critical mode's $1/\gamma$ gain law despite
their different detectors; the law does not describe every mode or the
entire finite-amplitude sweep.

\subsection{QIF responses distinguish beat modulation from field-induced gamma}\label{sec:qif}
The finite QIF network qualitatively reproduces beat-locked modulation at the lower tested
amplitude and field-induced gamma at the higher amplitude. Its $8000$-neuron populations test
these regimes at the spike level
\cite{montbrio2015,ruffini2025rosetta,clusella2023}
(Figs.~\ref{fig:qif_raster},~\ref{fig:qif_regimes}).

At $\bar\eta=0.85$ and $A_f=10$, the original finite-cutoff run gives
beat depth $0.09$ and a $2.8\%$ mean-rate increase. Its quantitative depth
remains unconverged because of cutoff and population sensitivity (supplement,
\S\ref{app:qif-checks}--\ref{supp:theta-pilot}). The estimated rectified
E-drive shift remains below $\delta\eta_E^*$, the shift in excitatory drive
required to cross the Hopf while holding the inhibitory drive fixed.
At $\bar\eta=1.5$, the original finite-cutoff run gives a $42$~Hz depth
of about $0.02$ and a $1.6\%$ mean-rate increase, while ongoing gamma dominates.

At $A_f=35$ and $\bar\eta=0.85$, a line near $52$~Hz dominates
(depth $0.70$), alongside the $55$~Hz beat (depth $0.29$). This is a
finite-amplitude example: carrier averaging predicts that the rectified
E-drive shift exceeds $\delta\eta_E^*$ and destabilizes the averaged equilibrium.
Direct mean-field integration reproduces the off-beat line, identifying the
dominant response as field-induced gamma (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_regimes}d).
The drive and stability estimates at both amplitudes are given in the
supplement (\S\ref{app:qif-estimate}).

At $\bar\eta=1.5$, autonomous gamma near $53$--$54$~Hz remains about
four times larger than the $42$~Hz beat component (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_raster}d).
Their coexistence produces slower burst-height variations. Mixing produces
sidebands near $95$ and $11$~Hz, as in endogenous amplitude modulation
\cite{ruffini2025ham}; these establish nonlinear mixing without distinguishing
gamma-amplitude gating from phase modulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}b).
Field-free gamma frequencies agree between the finite network and mean field
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}a).

To separate steady induction from beat-dependent activity, we compare TI
with an unmodulated carrier matched in RMS input current, in model units
(Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_regimes}c,d). In the mean field, their rectified E-drive
shifts agree within $3\%$ and their mean-rate increases are similar.
At $A_f=35$, both induce gamma near $51$~Hz; TI also produces the
$55$~Hz beat. At $A_f=10$, neither induces gamma, and TI alone produces
a resolved beat response. This control has been tested only in the mean
field; its spectral-leakage check is in the supplement
(\S\ref{supp:qif-protocol}).

At $A_f=35$, population beat modulation exceeds the relative mean-rate
change by about threefold. Across the two conditions, mean rate rises
$8$--$11\%$, while beat depth rises from below $0.003$ to $0.29$
($\bar\eta=0.85$) and $0.23$ ($\bar\eta=1.5$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:qif_timing}c).
Individual locking remains weak: median pairwise phase consistency rises from
$-0.02$ in the field-free control to $0.003$--$0.005$, and pooled vector
strength from below $0.02$ to $0.12$--$0.13$. Many weakly biased cells
therefore produce a population beat component. These finite-amplitude
results do not establish its size under weak physiological stimulation.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\subsection{Cellular access and mixing require separate calibration}
The JR and QIF models demonstrate local mixing followed by recurrent
amplification. Cellular mixing \cite{mirzakhalili2020,luff2024mixer} and network
involvement \cite{caldas2024,karimi2024,esmaeilpour2021} have complementary
experimental and modeling support, but their combined transfer at physiological
kHz fields remains unmeasured. The short-time cases in Table~\ref{tab:doors}
are access assumptions; varying the effective membrane time quantifies their
consequences.

At fields of order $1$~V/m, channels respond through membrane polarization,
well below fields associated with direct protein effects
\cite{cifra2026}.\footnote{That review places direct effects at $10$~kV/m
to GV/m, depending on molecule and protocol, and excludes fields below
$0.1$~kV/m, where it expects them to be negligible against thermal noise.}
Terminals can polarize more strongly than somata
\cite{rahman2013,aberra2023,chakraborty2018}. Small size alone does not imply
fast charging, since specific membrane resistance times capacitance is
area-independent; conductance, geometry, and stimulation mode determine access.
The supplementary comparison distinguishes these mechanisms from strong-pulse
charging (\S\ref{supp:access}).
\ifjne\else
\input{planning/parts/access_literature}
\fi

Missey et al.\ reported biomarker suppression with both low- and high-kHz carriers,
1300 and 1430~Hz at one center and 9000 and 9130~Hz at the others, per their
Methods \cite{missey2026}. For these pairs, the $0.2$~ms point-membrane
model predicts a high/low source ratio of $0.030$ at matched local fields. The
comparison challenges that access hypothesis but does not test it directly:
carrier band was confounded with center, local polarization was unmeasured, and a
nonlinear or saturating physiological readout could reconcile different sources
with similar biomarker changes.

\subsection{Detector laws predict different spatial responses}
The head-model comparison separates geometric consequences of detector laws:
the product and summed-squares maps differ from the envelope map even before
state-dependent detection and network gain enter. The fixed quadratic
pattern follows algebraically from fixed field shapes and linear access, whereas
opposite envelope and mean-square centroid motion is a result for the
tested subject and montages, not a general steering law. Because a sharp rectifier
can follow the envelope over a finite amplitude range while smoothing or
heterogeneity restores quadratic behavior at smaller inputs, amplitude and
harmonic sweeps complement the spatial comparison.

The connection to Violante et al.\ \cite{violante2023} is a hypothesis generated by these maps. Their models and cadaver recordings supported anterior envelope
steering from $2+2$~mA to $1+3$~mA. During participants' 32~s encoding blocks,
1:3 reduced the evoked blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) response relative
to sham ($P=0.006$) and 1:1 ($P=0.015$); 1:1 did not differ from sham
($P=0.953$). The stimulation-by-region interaction was nonsignificant
($P=0.76$), so the physiology did not establish spatial steering. Significant
voxelwise reductions extended beyond the anterior focus: 30\% of anterior,
25\% of posterior, and 2\% of middle hippocampal voxels.

In IXI002, mean-square polarization rises by 24\% under 1:3, while mean
envelope excursion falls by 45\% and both quadratic beat maps by 25\%.
At fixed uniform static curvature the steady-source magnitude therefore rises
by 24\%, whereas a mean shift from rectification of the beat scales as
$(3/4)^2\approx0.56$ in the weak-response limit. The first ordering is
consistent with stronger BOLD suppression if the steady source lowers activity
and the hemodynamic response preserves that ordering. A slow linear readout
suppresses a zero-mean $5$~Hz beat but retains a shift over the stimulation
window, and nonlinear neural or vascular responses can also convert that beat
into a mean change or alter the encoding response.

The comparison does not identify the biological detector. Local envelope excursion
can also increase under 1:3 (supplement, \S\ref{supp:steering-algebra}), allowing
a selective or thresholded envelope response. Equal summed currents do not match
local RMS polarization, and the nonsignificant 1:1--sham result does not establish
a null effect. Heterogeneous curvature and unknown recurrent and hemodynamic gains
can change the predicted ordering. Spatially resolved steady and beat responses,
measured at controlled polarization and state with an equal-RMS carrier control,
would discriminate the hypotheses.

\subsection{Field-induced oscillations and resonant amplification}
At stronger fields, TI and unmodulated carriers can have similar excitation
thresholds \cite{opancar2025,semenov2025}, and peripheral responses need not
extract the envelope \cite{peripheral2023}. Our weak-input calculation concerns
modulation of ongoing activity \cite{vieira2024}; the finite-amplitude QIF examples
instead show a mean shift crossing a circuit Hopf. That
boundary depends on amplitude and initial state and supplies no physiological
threshold. The supplementary laminar extension tests two resonant bands at low
carriers (\S\ref{app:lanmm}), outside TI's kHz regime.

The common TI/tACS amplifier follows from matching source, readout, and state, consistent with excitatory--inhibitory models \cite{ledoux2011,clusella2023} and related tACS/AM phase-locking studies \cite{ali2013,negahbani2018,krause2023}. Stimulation-induced state shifts can change the finite-amplitude ratio. The amplifier's temporal selectivity is a separate question. These models describe a single column, so their near-Hopf temporal slowing does not establish a diverging spatial correlation length. With reverberation time $\tau_{\rm rev}=1/\gamma$, the quality factor is $\mathcal Q=\pi f_0\tau_{\rm rev}$ and power bandwidth approximately $1/(\pi\tau_{\rm rev})$. Relaxation over a few hundred milliseconds \cite{wilting2018} gives order-ten quality and order-$1$~Hz bandwidth at $10$~Hz, making detuning consequential; that measured relaxation is not specific to an alpha mode.

\subsection{Efficiency depends on the generated drive}
The $2$--$5\%$ source comparison in \S\ref{sec:direct-comparison} matches each
TI carrier's polarization to the direct input. Matching the total TI peak instead halves the
ratio to $\mathcal R_{\rm det}\simeq|\Sig''|\varepsilon/(4\Sig')$; common
recurrent gain cancels in linear response.

Measured DC couplings are $0.12$--$0.18$~mV per V/m in hippocampus ($0.21$ at $10$~Hz)
\cite{bikson2004,deans2007} and reach $0.5$~mV per V/m in cortical L5/6 pyramidal
cells \cite{radman2009}; human cortical fields near $0.8$~V/m have been reported
at $2$~mA \cite{huang2017,huang2017corr}. These measurements constrain illustrative
input budgets, not the kHz transfer. For $E_j=0.5$~V/m,
$\lambda_0=0.5$~mV per V/m, $f_c=2$~kHz and the assumed $\tau_m=0.2$~ms, access
gives $\varepsilon\approx0.092$~mV. Rounding to $0.1$~mV yields
$\mathcal R_{\rm det}\approx0.8\%$ at $v^*=8.30$~mV under total-peak matching.
This is an assumed input budget, not an inferred polarization in recorded cells.

For balanced TI and a direct sinusoid matched in peak polarization at the same
operating point, the carrier-averaged open-loop fundamental-amplitude ratio
has a numerically estimated supremum of $0.348$ over bounded,
nondecreasing memoryless transfers (supplement, \S\ref{app:bound},
Eq.~\eqref{eq:etasup}). Dynamic QIF lies outside this detector class.

Vieira et al.'s weaker TI effects \cite{vieira2024} are compatible with a smaller
beat source, but their local mixer polarizations were unmatched (supplement,
\S\ref{supp:vieira}), and phase-locking value (PLV) also depends on baseline and
state. Estimating the detector coefficient requires a calibrated amplitude sweep
with a common readout.

\subsection{Limits of the population description}
Synaptic filters before a detector further attenuate its input. Model choice
also matters: Hodgkin--Huxley and Frankenhaeuser--Huxley models reproduce
stimulus-evoked firing that the tested leaky, exponential, and adaptive-exponential
integrate-and-fire models miss \cite{plovie2025}. Noise, additional heterogeneity,
and intercolumn interactions can broaden or shift our deterministic resonances.
Human findings include peripheral effects without detectable effects in the tested
central readouts \cite{iszak2023} and null occipito-parietal resting-alpha-power
modulation \cite{mansourinezhad2025}. Resting-state null results do not exclude
task-related alpha modulation, as reported by Thiele et al.\ \cite{thiele2024ttis}.
Access, generated sources, and circuit tuning therefore need measurement in the
same preparation.

\subsection{Falsifiable predictions and tests}\label{sec:predictions}
Interpreting timing and synaptic-blockade data requires independent measures of
amplitude scaling, harmonics, and recurrent gain (Table~\ref{tab:predictions}).

At resonance with
$\mathcal Q=10$, a detector second-harmonic/fundamental ratio of $20\%$ appears in
the measured response as only $0.7\%$, which could make an envelope follower look
quadratic. Driving off resonance at $\Delta f\simeq f_0/4$ places both harmonics on
the flatter part of the response, where $|G(2\Omega)/G(\Omega)|\approx1.22$--$1.25$
for $\mathcal Q=3$--$30$. Alternatively, a weak direct tACS sweep, calibrated for
local polarization and linear source conversion at the same operating point,
can estimate $G$ and correct the measured harmonic ratio.

\section{Conclusion}
Access, detection, and amplification determine different parts of the TI response. With nonzero curvature, a weak static
scalar detector generates a beat weighted by the carrier-polarization product
and a steady source weighted by their summed squares. The steady sign depends on the detector: static JR
curvature and fast-carrier QIF dynamics can give opposite signs. At fixed
field shapes and neural state, current-ratio changes rescale the quadratic beat map; the opposite
envelope and mean-square centroid shifts are specific to the head-model example.
With homogeneous conductivity and uniform scalar coupling and detection,
the steady-source magnitude has no strict interior maximum.

Near a Hopf bifurcation, TI and matched direct tACS share a resonant gain that
grows inversely with modal damping. In the pinned JR column this gain initially
favors beat modulation, while rectification of the amplified oscillation
increasingly contributes to the mean shift.
At stronger drive, a steady shift alone can induce gamma, including under an
equal-RMS unmodulated carrier in the mean field. Physiological kHz access remains
to be calibrated. Local-polarization measurements, independent-amplitude tests,
beat sweeps across states, and equal-RMS controls distinguish these mechanisms.
