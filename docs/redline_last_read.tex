\section{Introduction}
Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation was proposed to reach deep brain targets while
reducing stimulation of surrounding tissue \cite{grossman2017}. It applies two sinusoids at
$f_1$ and $f_2=f_1+\Delta f$ through separate electrode pairs, typically with
$f_1\sim1$--$2$~kHz and $\Delta f\sim1$--$100$~Hz. Where the fields overlap, their
superposition is an amplitude-modulated (AM) signal with an envelope at $\Delta f$.
Electrode currents can steer the region of largest envelope modulation. Whether this
also localizes the neural response depends on how neurons transduce the waveform.

The difference frequency is absent from the input itself:
\begin{equation}
s(t)=\varepsilon_1\cos(\omega_1 t)+\varepsilon_2\cos(\omega_2 t),\qquad
\omega_{1,2}=2\pi f_{1,2},\quad \Omega=\omega_2-\omega_1=2\pi\,\Delta f .
\label{eq:am}
\end{equation}
Here \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$s(t)$ is the local membrane polarization, $t$ is time, and
}\DIFaddend $\varepsilon_{1,2}$ are \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the effective input amplitudes }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{its tone amplitudes in mV }\DIFaddend (related to the applied field in \S\ref{sec:coupling}), $\Omega$ is the beat angular frequency, and
$f_c=(f_1+f_2)/2$ is the carrier frequency. The spectrum contains only the two applied
lines (Fig.~\ref{fig:demod}). A linear, time-invariant system can attenuate or phase-shift
them but cannot create a new one. Passive membrane filtering therefore cannot
demodulate TI \cite{mirzakhalili2020}; a nonlinear element must mix the carriers into
the low-frequency band, as in AM reception \cite{nahin2001}.

The mechanism therefore has distinct physical requirements (Fig.~\ref{fig:concept}).
\emph{Access} is the conversion of the extracellular field into local membrane
polarization. \emph{Detection}, or demodulation, is the nonlinear generation of the
difference-frequency component from those polarizations. \emph{Amplification} is
the response of the neuron and its recurrent circuit to the generated component.
The same cellular structures can contribute to more than one stage, but strong
coupling, effective mixing, and large resonant gain are different properties.

channels. These measurements establish a cellular detector without locating it
within a specific compartment. In a population description, smooth cellular
contributions can be combined into an effective second-order response; a static
rate curve realizes that response through its curvature.

\subsection{Population transfer and the slow-synapse limit}\label{sec:nmm}
A neural mass model describes a population through averaged variables
\cite{ruffini2025rosetta,jansen1995}. In the Jansen--Rit (JR) family, a linear synaptic
filter converts presynaptic firing rate $r(t)$ into postsynaptic potential \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$x(t)$:
}\[
\DIFdel{\ddot x+2a\dot x+a^2x=Aa\,r(t),
}\]%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$u_{\rm PSP}(t)$:
}\[
\DIFadd{\ddot u_{\rm PSP}+2a\dot u_{\rm PSP}+a^2u_{\rm PSP}=Aa\,r(t),
}\]\DIFaddend
with inverse synaptic time $a$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and gain parameter }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(s$^{-1}$) and PSP gain }\DIFaddend $A$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(mV)}\DIFaddend ; inhibitory synapses use
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{parameters }\DIFdelend $b$ and $B$ \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{in the same respective units}\DIFaddend . Summed postsynaptic potentials give the mean input $v$, which
a static transfer curve converts to firing rate \cite{wilsoncowan1972},
\begin{equation}
\Sig(v)=\frac{2e_0}{1+e^{\,\rho(v_0-v)}} .
\label{eq:sigmoid}
\end{equation}

voltage scale \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(s$^{-1}$, mV, and mV$^{-1}$, respectively). Primes denote
derivatives with respect to voltage. }\DIFaddend Nonzero curvature makes this curve a mixer, while its slope sets
the local gain of the recurrent circuit.

For a synapse from population $Y$ to $X$, the resulting first-generation neural
mass model (NMM1) equations are

\begin{equation}

\label{eq:nmm1}
\end{equation}
The operator $\hat L$ is the inverse synaptic filter, $u_{X\leftarrow Y}$ its

the direct polarization caused by the applied field $E$ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{(V/m). The rates $r_X,r_Y$

alongside the synaptic potentials, without first passing through a synapse.

slow input into a rate change and enters recurrent gain. QIF supplies a derived
realization through its spike-generating quadratic and excitability distribution.

model-specific. Outside the static limit these contributions belong to a dynamic
input--output functional. Section~\ref{sec:dynamic} calculates this response for
QIF and recovers Eq.~\eqref{eq:Fmpr} in the adiabatic limit.

\subsection{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Transduction class }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Detector laws }\DIFaddend and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{its spatial and spectral }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{harmonic }\DIFaddend signatures}\label{sec:transduction}
A neural response proportional to the squared envelope is not equivalent to one
proportional to the envelope. A detector implements some transduction \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$T(A)$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$T(\mathcal A)$ }\DIFaddend of the local
envelope \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A(t)$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A(t)$}\DIFaddend , and the two laws predict different amplitude scaling, harmonic content and
spatial weighting.

For a narrowband field \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$s(t)=A(t)\cos\omega_c t$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$s(t)=\mathcal A(t)\cos\omega_c t$ }\DIFaddend passing through a memoryless nonlinearity
$g$, the low-frequency (baseband) output $Y_{\rm LF}$ is the average of $g$ over one
carrier cycle\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{:
}\[
\DIFdel{Y_{\rm LF}(A)=(2\pi)^{-1}\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!g(v^*+A\cos\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
}\]%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, with carrier phase $\theta$:
}\[
\DIFadd{Y_{\rm LF}(\mathcal A)=(2\pi)^{-1}\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!g(v^*+\mathcal A\cos\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.
}\]\DIFaddend
Odd powers of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A$ }\DIFaddend average away, leaving only even ones:
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{Y_{\rm LF}(A)=g(v^*)+\tfrac14 g''(v^*)A^2+\tfrac1{64}g^{(4)}(v^*)A^4+\cdots
%DIFDELCMD < %%%
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \begin{equation}
\DIFadd{Y_{\rm LF}(\mathcal A)=g(v^*)+\tfrac14 g''(v^*)\mathcal A^2+\tfrac1{64}g^{(4)}(v^*)\mathcal A^4+\cdots
\label{eq:cycavg}
}\end{equation}\DIFaddend
A smooth memoryless response with $g''(v^*)\neq0$ therefore transduces \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A^2$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A^2$ }\DIFaddend at
leading weak-input order, whatever its detailed shape. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{This includes the sigmoid
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{For the JR column, $g=\Sig$, giving the
}\DIFaddend mechanism of \S\ref{sec:coupling}. Recovering \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A$ }\DIFaddend itself requires a qualitatively
different detector: strong rectification. A full-wave rectifier \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{gives
$\mathrm{LPF}[\,|A\cos\omega_c t|\,]=(2/\pi)A$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{has low-pass-filtered (LPF) output
$\mathrm{LPF}[\,|\mathcal A\cos\omega_c t|\,]=(2/\pi)\mathcal A$ }\DIFaddend and a half-wave rectifier
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$(1/\pi)A$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$(1/\pi)\mathcal A$}\DIFaddend , both linear in the envelope, as does a diode-and-capacitor peak detector.
A hard threshold is not one of them: a step detector with offset $\delta>0$ transduces
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\pi^{-1}\arccos(\delta/A)$ for $A>\delta$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\pi^{-1}\arccos(\delta/\mathcal A)$ for $\mathcal A>\delta$ }\DIFaddend and nothing below it, which is neither linear
nor quadratic in \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A$ }\DIFaddend (App.~\ref{app:bound}). Asymmetric channel rectification, threshold
crossing and release events are candidate biological realizations of rectifiers of one
kind or another.

Envelope following and square-law detection are distinguishable at a single site. At a matched target,
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A(t)=2\varepsilon|\cos(\Omega t/2)|$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal A(t)=2\varepsilon|\cos(\Omega t/2)|$}\DIFaddend , whose Fourier series
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$|\cos u|=\tfrac2\pi+\tfrac4\pi\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{4n^2-1}\cos 2nu$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$|\cos\theta|=\tfrac2\pi+\tfrac4\pi\sum_{n\ge1}\tfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{4n^2-1}\cos(2n\theta)$ }\DIFaddend gives a
harmonic ladder \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{in ratios }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{whose nonnegative Fourier amplitudes $A_{n\Omega}$ obey }\DIFaddend $A_{2\Omega}/A_\Omega=1/5$, $A_{3\Omega}/A_\Omega=3/35$ and
$A_{4\Omega}/A_\Omega=1/21$. The square-law output \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\half A^2=\varepsilon^2(1+\cos\Omega
t)$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\half \mathcal A^2=\varepsilon^2(1+\cos\Omega
t)$ }\DIFaddend contains only DC and $\Omega$. For the mixed family \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$T(A)=c_1A+c_2A^2$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$T(\mathcal A)=c_1\mathcal A+c_2\mathcal A^2$}\DIFaddend , with
$c_1\ne0$ and a nonzero fundamental, the
pre-network ratio is
\DIFdelbegin \begin{displaymath}
\DIFdel{r_{\rm det}\equiv\left|\frac{Y_{2\Omega}}{Y_\Omega}\right|

%DIFDELCMD < %%%
}\end{displaymath}%DIFAUXCMD

\DIFadd{\mathcal H_{\rm det}\equiv\left|\frac{Y_{2\Omega}}{Y_\Omega}\right|

}\end{equation}\DIFaddend
so measuring \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$r_{\rm det}$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\mathcal H_{\rm det}$ }\DIFaddend constrains $c_2\varepsilon/c_1$ within this family.\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{The inverse has two branches unless the sign of the fundamental relative to

}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \footnote{\DIFadd{The inverse has two branches unless the sign of the fundamental

A smooth detector generates a second harmonic beyond quadratic order,
$Y_{2\Omega}/Y_\Omega\sim(c_4/c_2)\varepsilon^2$, where
$c_2=g''(v^*)/4$ and $c_4=g^{(4)}(v^*)/64$ are the coefficients in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cycavg}. The square-law signature is a
\emph{vanishing} ratio in the weak-field limit, not an exact zero. The recurrent
network also multiplies each harmonic by its \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{own gain ,
$r_{\rm meas}=r_{\rm det}\,|G(2\Omega)/G(\Omega)|$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{frequency-dependent gain $G$,
$\mathcal H_{\rm meas}=\mathcal H_{\rm det}\,|G(2\Omega)/G(\Omega)|$}\DIFaddend .
Near resonance, harmonic suppression can make an envelope follower appear square-law.

An amplitude sweep tests the scaling rather than one ratio. An
envelope-following detector gives $Y_\Omega\propto\varepsilon$ with the harmonic ratio
pinned at $1/5$; a weak square-law detector gives $Y_\Omega\propto\varepsilon^2$ with a
ratio growing as $\varepsilon^2$. A threshold detector is a third class: its
response vanishes for $2\varepsilon\le\delta$, and its harmonic ratio varies
with amplitude after onset. A single harmonic ratio cannot distinguish all
three; amplitude dependence is the primary test.

\subsection{Carrier access depends on the effective membrane response}\label{sec:khz}

cable, rather than a measured transfer from a TI field. The AIS is another candidate
site, with a high sodium-channel density \cite{kole2008ais}; this property supports
nonlinear sensitivity but does not fix its charging time.

\subsection{Operating-point dependence}\label{sec:opp}
Because \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$A_\Omega\propto\Sig''(v^*)$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$Y_\Omega\propto\Sig''(v^*)$}\DIFaddend , demodulation is governed by the curvature at
the operating point, and in particular by how far $v^*$ sits from the sigmoid
inflection (Fig.~\ref{fig:opp}). The Population Excitation--Inhibition Index (PEIX)
of \cite{ruffini2025cfc} measures signed departure from maximal sigmoid slope:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PEIX}(v^*)=\operatorname{sign}(v^*-v_0)
\left\{1-\operatorname{sech}^2\!\Big[\tfrac{\rho}{2}\,(v^*-v_0)\Big]\right\}.
\label{eq:peix}
\end{equation}
It is zero at $v_0$, positive above it, negative below it, and tends to $\pm1$
in the saturated tails. The normalized slope is instead
$\Sig'(v^*)/\Sig'(v_0)=1-|\mathrm{PEIX}(v^*)|$, with
$\Sig'(v_0)=e_0\rho/2$. Demodulation follows the curvature, which vanishes at
the inflection and in the tails and peaks in magnitude on the flanks. For the
logistic curve,
$\Sig''=-\rho\Sig'\operatorname{sign}(\mathrm{PEIX})\sqrt{|\mathrm{PEIX}|}$;
the index specifies the side and saturation, but is not itself the mixing gain.

This relation is specific to the memoryless logistic detector. In the exact mean field at a fast carrier the rectified DC term
follows the slope $F'$ and is largest at the inflection (\S\ref{sec:dynamic}), so the
relation between PEIX and TI efficacy is model-dependent; PEIX remains a measurable
read-out of the operating point. In the Jansen--Rit detector, anything shifting $v^*$
(\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{for example, }\DIFaddend neuromodulation, attention, arousal, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{or pathology such as psychedelics or }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{psychedelic exposure, or
}\DIFaddend PV-interneuron dysfunction in Alzheimer's disease) scales, and can flip the sign of, the TI response; in
the dynamic detector the same shifts act through $F'$ and the susceptibility, without a
sign rule. The state dependence is the falsifiable handle; the sign reversal is specific
to the memoryless model.

\subsection{Vocabulary: forced response, entrainment, and gating}\DIFaddbegin \label{sec:vocab-methods}
\DIFaddend \emph{Forced response} denotes the component driven at the beat $\Delta f$,
quantified by the lock-in amplitude (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lockin}). A purely forced regime
requires a stable operating state under stimulation; being below Hopf with the field
off is insufficient. \emph{Entrainment} is reserved for its
dynamical-systems meaning, frequency capture of a \emph{self-sustained} oscillator
\cite{pikovsky2001}; in the maps we screen for it with the dominance criterion
$|f_{\rm out}-\Delta f|<0.3$~Hz on the dominant output frequency. This identifies
frequency dominance, not phase capture. \emph{Gating} is amplitude modulation of a persisting rhythm without
frequency capture \cite{canolty2010,tort2010}. Capture and gating are dynamically different, and the same word would otherwise cover
both. The QIF rate fold measures beat-locked firing, which does not by itself
establish gating of gamma amplitude. Appendix~\ref{sec:vocab} gives the metrics
and the frequency-dominance protocol.

\DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The coupling sweeps approach the Hopf from field-free stable foci ($\gamma>0$), so the
$1/\gamma$ law concerns forced susceptibility. The frequency-dominance maps on
the oscillatory side describe a different observable: their bands broaden with
drive, but an Arnold tongue describes phase capture of the autonomous limit
cycle and requires phase tracking (\S\ref{sec:vocab}).
}
