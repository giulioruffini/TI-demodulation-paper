\newcommand{\absObjective}{We develop a mesoscale model of temporal-interference (TI) stimulation with three stages
in series: membrane access, nonlinear detection, and circuit amplification.}

\newcommand{\absApproach}{Access is represented by a short effective membrane time. We analyze detection and
amplification in a Jansen--Rit column, an exact mean field of quadratic
integrate-and-fire neurons, and its spiking network, and test spatial predictions in a
realistic head.}

\newcommand{\absResults}{For weak fields, quadratic detection generates a beat proportional to the product of
local carrier polarizations and a signed, DC-like steady source proportional to their
summed squares. Direct transcranial alternating- and direct-current stimulation (tACS
and tDCS) sources are linear in polarization to leading order. At the Jansen--Rit
operating point studied, TI carrier polarizations of 0.1--0.3 mV generate steady and
beat sources approximately 2--5\% as large as those generated by tDCS and tACS,
respectively, when the direct-input polarization amplitude equals that of each TI
carrier. Membranes slow relative to input add frequency dependence. With a hippocampal
montage, changing the current ratio moves envelope and mean-square foci oppositely but
only rescales the beat map at fixed state. Near a Hopf bifurcation, resonant gain grows
inversely with modal damping and is shared with tACS. At a fixed Jansen--Rit operating
point, coupling changes gain independently of detection; amplification appears mainly in
beat-locked timing, with little mean-rate change. Stronger steady sources can drive a
gamma circuit across its oscillation threshold, as can an equal-power unmodulated
carrier.}

\newcommand{\absSignificance}{Weak-field beat dose follows the polarization product rather than the envelope minimum.
The steady source can act at depth, its sign set by curvature, not polarity. Its
magnitude has no strict interior maximum within tissue regions of uniform conductivity, access,
orientation, and detection. Polarization measurements, amplitude and frequency sweeps,
and equal-power controls distinguish the stages and separate steady-source effects from
those requiring the beat.}
