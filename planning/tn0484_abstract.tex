Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation combines two high-frequency currents, yet their
superposition contains no field component at the low difference frequency. Population
models generate that component through effective quadratic mixing and amplify it through
recurrent resonance. Our focus is weak perturbations of ongoing activity. For a smooth
response with a nonzero second-order term,
cellular nonlinearities contribute to a beat proportional to the product of the carrier
amplitudes. A Jansen--Rit firing-rate sigmoid realizes this mixing through its curvature.
An exact quadratic integrate-and-fire mean field derives this description from spiking
dynamics. Its static rate curve emerges when membrane relaxation is fast relative to the
input, while synapses remain dynamic. Outside that limit the mixing coefficient depends
on frequency. Fast cellular access and slow circuit resonance can therefore coexist, with
membrane attenuation entering once, through access. Near a Hopf bifurcation, the network amplifies the
generated beat as $1/\gamma$ within linear response, where $\gamma$ is the modal damping.
Coupling sweeps separate this gain from leading-order beat generation. Near resonance,
rate modulation grows faster than the mean-rate change. Finite-amplitude simulations in
networks of $8000$ neurons per population also show a beat-locked component alongside gamma
activity. In the weak-input regime, the carrier-product
law makes an envelope-only dose map conditional on the transduction mechanism. TI and
direct low-frequency stimulation share the amplifier when effective input coupling,
readout, and operating state are matched, so resonance alone gives TI no efficiency
advantage. Biological access and the absolute response at human field strengths remain
unresolved.
