\newcommand{\absObjective}{Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation can act on neural
populations only if three stages work in series: the kilohertz field must reach a
nonlinear membrane (access), the membrane must generate the difference frequency
absent from the field (detection), and the circuit must amplify that weak component
(amplification). We build a mesoscale model that represents all three stages and
derive what each contributes.}

\newcommand{\absApproach}{Following single-neuron TI models that locate rectification
in nodes and the axon initial segment, we represent access by a short effective
membrane time. We analyze detection and amplification in a Jansen--Rit column, an
exact mean field of quadratic integrate-and-fire neurons, and the corresponding
spiking network.}

\newcommand{\absResults}{For weak fields the detector is quadratic. The beat is
proportional to the product of the two local carrier amplitudes and is accompanied
by a steady operating-point shift proportional to their summed squares. A static
rate curve and the exact mean field give the same product law, and the mean field
adds frequency dependence when the membrane is slow relative to the input. Near a
Hopf bifurcation the circuit amplifies the beat as the inverse of the modal damping,
and synaptic coupling sets this gain independently of detection in the Jansen--Rit
column with its field-free operating point held fixed. The amplified response
appears mainly in envelope-locked timing, with a much smaller change in mean rate.
At stronger fields the steady shift alone can drive a gamma circuit across its
oscillation threshold, and an unmodulated carrier of equal power does the same.}

\newcommand{\absSignificance}{The model expresses TI efficacy as the product of three
separately measurable factors. In the weak-field regime, dose follows the product
of carrier amplitudes rather than the envelope minimum, the steady shift cannot be
focused by the montage within a homogeneous tissue compartment with uniform access
and detection, and TI shares its circuit amplifier with direct low-frequency
stimulation. Carrier sweeps, independent-amplitude tests, beat sweeps across states,
and equal-power controls each test one stage.}
