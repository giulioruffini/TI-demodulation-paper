Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation applies two high-frequency currents whose
superposition contains no field component at their low difference frequency. Neural
populations can generate that component by nonlinear mixing and amplify it selectively
by recurrent resonance. In a Jansen--Rit column, the firing-rate sigmoid mixes the
carriers through its curvature. In an exact quadratic integrate-and-fire mean field, the
corresponding static curve emerges when the membrane variables relax faster than their
input while the synapses remain dynamic; at faster inputs, mixing depends on the
population dynamics and cannot be read from the steady-state curve. Fast cellular access
and slow circuit resonance can therefore coexist in one population model. In both
descriptions the leading weak-input beat is proportional to the product of the effective
carrier amplitudes, and the membrane's attenuation of the carrier enters that product
once, through those amplitudes. Recurrent dynamics near a Hopf bifurcation amplify the
beat as $1/\gamma$, where $\gamma$ is the damping of the critical mode. Connectivity
controls this gain at fixed generated drive, and beat-locked rate modulation grows
faster than the mean-rate change, in the mean field and in a spiking network of $8000$
neurons per population. Because the beat follows the carrier product rather than the
envelope excursion, an envelope-only dose map rests on a physiological assumption. TI
and direct low-frequency stimulation share the downstream amplifier, so resonance alone
confers no TI efficiency advantage. The biological access pathway and the absolute
response at human field strengths remain unresolved.
