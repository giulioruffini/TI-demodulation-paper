\pagetitle{Problem and theory}{First argument block: Sections 1--2}
A two-tone field carries no power at its beat frequency, so a neural response there requires a
nonlinear element. The question is how much of that response belongs to the population circuit
rather than to the cell. The theory builds the receiver in three stages, access, mixing, and
amplification, and shows that the static and dynamic detectors are one object seen at
different carrier speeds.

\place{\S1}{Introduction}
& The two-tone field (Eq.~1) has lines only at the applied tones; a linear system cannot create
  the beat. Cellular mixing exists (MRG and Hodgkin--Huxley axon models; Luff's subthreshold
  mixer at 5~kHz, TTX-sensitive). Network dependence exists (timing-not-rate in primates;
  pyramidal responses lost under synaptic block; network simulations). The paper separates
  carrier access, beat generation, and recurrent amplification, and the population
  reduction does not identify the anatomical access route.
& States the problem and the distinction the paper turns on: cellular mixing versus the
  network response it recruits. Announces the ladder (JR column, QIF mean field, spiking
  network, laminar column) once. The transfer curve must be defined before its curvature
  can mix, so \S2.1 follows.
& Fig.~1 (receiver concept). Grossman 2017; Mirzakhalili 2020; Luff 2024 (mixer); Vieira
  2024; Caldas-Martinez 2024; CFC and HAM companion papers.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.1}{Population transfer and the slow-synapse limit}
& JR synaptic filter and sigmoid (Eqs.~2--3); NMM1 with the direct field term
  $\Lambda[\hat E]$ (Eq.~3). The MPR mean field (Eq.~4) and its static curve $F(Q)$ (Eq.~5),
  inflection at $Q=\Delta/\sqrt3$. The slow-synapse reduction (Eq.~6) and its two conditions:
  $\tau_{\rm rel}\ll\tau_s$ makes the transfer static for synaptic input; $\omega_c\tau_{\rm rel}\ll1$
  is the stronger condition for a carrier. The QIF reduction does not contain sodium-channel
  kinetics. Cellular and circuit resonance are distinct.
& Defines what a transfer curve is and when it is static, so that curvature can be a mixer.
  Introduces the time-scale distinction that the Discussion and Conclusion reuse. The field
  must reach the curve, which \S2.2 supplies.
& Rosetta Stone (Ruffini 2025); Montbri\'o 2015; Clusella 2023; de Palma Aristides 2026
  (WP0242, unpublished); Hutcheon and Yarom 2000.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.2}{From applied field to a squared-envelope beat}
& Quasistatic field; polarization $s(t)$ through parallel entry points with access filters
  (Eqs.~7--8), one compartment response counted once. Taylor expansion (Eqs.~9--10) with the
  truncation condition $|s|\ll1/\rho\approx1.8$~mV. The square of the envelope (Eq.~11): $A^2$

  Galan and Gadea 2023.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.3}{The exact mean field connects static and dynamic mixing}
& $C(Q,\omega)$ (Eq.~13) with limits $F''/4$ and $F'/(2\omega^2)$ (Eq.~14), sign change at
  $\omega_z$ above the inflection. Fixed-excursion coefficient $K(\omega)$ (Eq.~15): the
  $1/\omega^2$ is the membrane, not a detector filter. The PING $v_E^2$ source matches the
  second-order response to 1.1\%, 0.1\%, 0.03\% at 300, 1000, 2000~Hz. Carrier and beat take
  different routes to the rate (Table~1).
& Shows that the static and dynamic detectors are one object at different carrier speeds,
  with membrane access counted once; settles the detector-penalty question of September 2026.
  Whatever the detector, its envelope law has observable signatures, which \S2.4 derives.
& Fig.~7; Table~1. Code: \path{static_vs_dynamic_check.py}, \path{mpr_twotone_lines_check.py}.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.4}{Transduction class and its signatures}
& Cycle average (Eq.~16) is even in $A$; square law is generic for a smooth transfer;
  rectifiers follow $A$; a step detector follows neither. Harmonic ladder $1/5$, $3/35$, $1/21$
  and $r_{\rm det}$ (Eq.~17). The network suppresses the second harmonic ($\mathcal Q=10$:
  20\% reads 0.7\%), so the test runs off resonance at $\Delta f\simeq f_0/4$. Excursion map
  (Eq.~18): product law, $2\times$ versus $40\times$ example; not an account of focality. Spatial
  response (Eq.~19) and signed vector coupling.
& Converts the detector class into measurements (amplitude slope, harmonic ratio, dose map)
  and separates spatial from dynamical selectivity. Generation gives a weak drive; \S2.5
  explains what makes it large.
& App.~D (bound and step detector); Fig.~6b (slope 1.99); Table~3 (predictions).\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.5}{Amplification: synaptic low-pass and network resonance}
& Synaptic transfer $H_s$ (16~Hz corner; 0.7 at 10~Hz, 2.5\% at 100~Hz). One-mode reduction,
  $\mu_\pm=-\gamma\pm i\omega_0$, $\mathcal Q=\omega_0/2\gamma$, gain $\propto1/\gamma$
  (Eq.~20); entrainment above the Hopf. Receiver equation (Eq.~21) with $D$ and $G$ and their
  normalization for JR and QIF. The cubic normal form has no quadratic mixer.
& Supplies the second factor of the receiver and the claim that a large susceptibility is
  not evidence of detection. The carrier must still reach the mixer, which \S2.6 quantifies.
& Fig.~5; Figs.~4 and 9; App.~C.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.6}{Carrier access depends on the effective membrane response}
& $H_m$ (Eq.~22); $|H_m|^2\approx10^{-4}$ at 1~kHz for 16~ms, 0.39 and 0.14 at 1 and 2~kHz for

  at 1~kHz. A gate filter needs its own model; the QIF coefficient is not a calibrated sodium
  response. Carrier sweeps constrain access plus mixing; beat sweeps probe the amplifier.
& Separates carrier independence at fixed polarization from the applied-field roll-off and

  which \S2.7 takes up.
& Figs.~S5, S6, S9; Deitcher 2017; Eyal 2016; Discussion paragraph on Cohen, Luff, Semenov.\\[2pt]

\place{\S2.7}{Operating-point dependence}
& PEIX (Eq.~24) tracks $\Sigma'$ and peaks where $\Sigma''$ vanishes; the complementarity

  no sign rule. State shifts (neuromodulation, pathology) scale, and in JR can flip, the response.
& Turns the detector coefficient into a brain-state prediction and marks which part is
  model-specific. The theory is complete; Methods specify the simulations that test it.
& Fig.~2b; Fig.~6a; CFC paper (PEIX).\\[2pt]

\pagetitle{Simulations}{Second argument block: Sections 3--4}
The Results climb the ladder. The JR column verifies the three predictions of \S2.2; the exact
mean field reproduces generation and amplification with a derived nonlinearity; the spiking
network confirms the timing signature at the microscale. Methods stand apart because the
lock-in read-out and the pinned-operating-point protocol recur across models.
