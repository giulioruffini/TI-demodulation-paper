\newcommand{\absObjective}{Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation can act on neural
populations only if three stages work in series: the kilohertz field must reach a
nonlinear membrane (access), the membrane must generate the difference frequency
absent from the field (detection), and the circuit must amplify that weak component
(amplification). We build a mesoscale model of all three stages.}

\newcommand{\absApproach}{Following single-neuron TI models that locate rectification
in nodes and the axon initial segment, we represent access by a short effective
membrane time. We analyze detection and amplification in a Jansen--Rit column, an
exact mean field of quadratic integrate-and-fire neurons, and its spiking network.}

\newcommand{\absResults}{For weak fields the detector is quadratic: the beat is
proportional to the product of the local carrier amplitudes, and a steady
operating-point shift to their summed squares. A static rate curve and the exact
mean field give the same product law; the mean field adds frequency dependence
when the membrane is slow relative to the input. Near a Hopf bifurcation the
circuit amplifies the beat as the inverse of the modal damping; with the
Jansen--Rit operating point held fixed, synaptic coupling sets this gain
independently of detection. The amplified response appears mainly in
envelope-locked timing, with little change in mean rate.
At stronger fields the steady shift alone can drive a gamma circuit across its
oscillation threshold, and an unmodulated carrier of equal power does the same.}

\newcommand{\absSignificance}{The model expresses TI efficacy as the product of three
separately measurable factors. For weak fields, dose follows the product of carrier
amplitudes rather than the envelope minimum. Under uniform conductivity, access,
and detection, the steady drive has its largest magnitude at compartment boundaries. TI shares
its circuit amplifier with direct low-frequency stimulation. Carrier sweeps,
independent-amplitude tests, beat sweeps across states, and equal-power controls
each test one stage.}
