Temporal-interference (TI) stimulation combines two high-frequency currents, yet
their superposition contains no field component at the low difference frequency.
The neural response requires field-to-membrane access, nonlinear detection, and
amplification. Single-neuron TI models already demonstrate axonal ion-channel
rectification. We represent candidate fast access through axonal compartments,
including nodes and the axon initial segment, by a short effective membrane time
in the population model. Periaxonal circuitry motivates separate axonal and
somatic voltage responses.
The field-to-voltage transfer and response at human field strengths require calibration.
For weak perturbations of ongoing activity, smooth cellular nonlinearities with
a nonzero second-order response generate a beat proportional to the product of
the effective carrier amplitudes. A Jansen--Rit firing-rate sigmoid realizes this
mixing through its curvature. An exact quadratic integrate-and-fire mean field
connects the static curve to spiking dynamics: fast membrane relaxation leaves
dynamic synapses, whereas outside that limit mixing depends on frequency.
Membrane attenuation enters once, through access. Near a Hopf bifurcation, the
network amplifies the generated beat as $1/\gamma$ within linear response, where
$\gamma$ is modal damping. Coupling sweeps separate this gain from leading-order
beat generation. Rate modulation grows faster than the mean-rate change near
resonance. Spiking simulations distinguish beat modulation below the oscillatory
threshold from gamma induced when rectification crosses it; an equal-power
mean-field carrier control separates induction from the beat response.
The weak-input product law makes envelope-only dosimetry conditional on
transduction. Within linear response, TI and direct low-frequency stimulation
share the amplifier when coupling, readout, and state are matched, so resonance
alone gives TI no efficiency advantage.
